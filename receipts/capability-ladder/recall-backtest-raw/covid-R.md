The concept:

When a person breathes, talks, coughs, or sneezes, they release many small blobs of watery fluid of widely varying sizes. Each blob's fate is set mostly by its diameter. Large blobs fall to the ground quickly under gravity within a short distance. Small blobs lose their water so fast that they shrink to tiny dry specks before they can fall; these specks are light enough to stay suspended in the air for minutes to hours and drift with air currents well beyond the person who released them. There is a crossover diameter, depending on how dry the surrounding air is, that separates "falls to the ground" from "shrinks and stays aloft". Question: as a function of a blob's starting diameter (and the humidity), how long does it stay in the air and how far can it travel before it either lands or dries to a suspended speck, and what body of work first worked out this size-versus-fate relationship?
