## Identification

**1. What it's called**

There is no single unified academic term for the whole operational rule you describe — it's actually two different things wearing one name, which is the crux of the story:

- **"Droplet precautions" vs. "Airborne precautions"** (or "droplet transmission" vs. "airborne/aerosol transmission") — the operational, guideline-level term used in infection control (CDC, WHO, hospital epidemiology).
- **The "5 μm cut-off"** or **"droplet–aerosol size threshold"** — the specific numeric claim underlying it.
- **The "Wells evaporation–falling curve"** (or **"Wells curve"**) — the underlying physics model these guidelines claim to be based on.
- In the aerosol-science/infection-control literature that has spent the last ~5-10 years dismantling it, it's referred to as **the "droplet–aerosol dichotomy"** or **"the 5 μm myth."**

**2. Which field(s) own it**

Two fields co-own it, uneasily:
- **Clinical infection control / hospital epidemiology** (CDC/HICPAC, WHO) — owns the operational guideline (droplet vs. airborne precautions, PPE, room ventilation).
- **Aerosol science / fluid dynamics of disease transmission** — owns the physics of droplet evaporation, settling, and airflow that the guideline claims to rest on.
- A third, historical field — **aerobiology and industrial/occupational hygiene** (1930s–40s lung-deposition studies) — is where the actual "5 μm" number originated, for an unrelated question (what size penetrates to the alveoli), and got fused into the transmission-route guideline by mistake. This mis-fusion is the specific thing your question is asking about.

**Confidence: high** on this framing — it's explicit in the primary historical source below, not my inference.

**3. Answering your three sub-questions directly**

- **How far do respiratory droplets travel?** Not a fixed 1–2 m. It depends on droplet size, expiratory violence (breathing/talking vs. coughing/sneezing), humidity, and ambient airflow — droplets are ejected inside a turbulent, humid gas cloud/jet that can carry even "large" droplets several meters beyond where still-air physics would predict, and small droplets can stay suspended for minutes to hours and travel with room air currents far beyond a few meters. Bourouiba (2020, JAMA) documented sneeze/cough gas clouds carrying droplets up to ~7–8 m. Xie et al. (2007, *Indoor Air*) found that under typical coughing conditions, most droplet sizes settle or evaporate within ~2 m, but the exact distance is highly sensitive to humidity (an ~80 cm difference for 30 μm droplets between 30% and 70% RH) — i.e., "1–2 m" is a rough empirical average, not a physical constant.
- **Where does the droplet-vs.-airborne line actually fall?** The physically derived cutoff — from Wells' own 1934 evaporation/settling calculations, the transition between droplets that fall to the ground faster than they evaporate versus those that evaporate faster than they fall — is **~100 μm**, not 5 μm. Modern aerosol scientists further argue there is no sharp physical cutoff at all: exhaled particles form a size continuum whose airborne fate depends on evaporation and airflow conditions, not a single threshold. **Confidence: high**, directly stated and derived in the primary sources below.
- **What established the (5 μm) line actually used in guidelines?** It was not established by any transmission/distance study. It traces to 1930s–40s industrial-hygiene/inhalation research on what particle size penetrates to the alveoli (unrelated to whether a particle stays airborne), which Alexander Langmuir — first Chief Epidemiologist of the CDC's predecessor — conflated with Wells' droplet/droplet-nuclei framework in a 1951 address on biological warfare and again in a 1964 paper, after which it was codified into CDC/WHO infection-control guidance (e.g., CDC's 1996/2007 Isolation Precautions guideline). **Confidence: high** that this is the documented mechanism, per Randall et al. (2021) below, who traced the citation trail explicitly.

**Canonical citations (oldest first):**

- Wells WF. **"On air-borne infection. Study II: Droplets and droplet nuclei."** *American Journal of Hygiene*, 1934, 20(3):611–618. — Foundational physics paper; establishes the evaporation-falling curve and the actual ~100 μm size cutoff. *(Confidence: high — verified via Oxford Academic listing and multiple secondary confirmations.)*
- Langmuir AD. **"The potentialities of biological warfare against man: an epidemiological appraisal."** *Public Health Reports*, 1951, 66:387–399. doi:10.2307/4587679. — The address where the 5 μm alveolar-deposition figure gets pulled into the airborne-infection discussion. *(Confidence: high — verified via PMC/PubMed and quoted directly in Randall et al.)*
- Wells WF. **Airborne Contagion and Air Hygiene: An Ecological Study of Droplet Infections.** Harvard University Press, 1955. — Wells' own later book, where he references the 1–5 μm alveolar-deposition literature alongside his droplet-nuclei theory, likely the point where the two ideas visibly merge. *(Confidence: medium — bibliographic details confirmed via Google Books/secondary sourcing, not independently read cover-to-cover.)*
- Langmuir AD. **"Airborne infection: how important for public health?"** *American Journal of Public Health*, 1964, 54:1666–1668. — Explicitly uses the 5 μm large-droplet/aerosol distinction tied to lung deposition. *(Confidence: high — verified via PMC/PubMed.)*
- Siegel JD, Rhinehart E, Jackson M, Chiarello L, and the Healthcare Infection Control Practices Advisory Committee (CDC/HICPAC). **"2007 Guideline for Isolation Precautions: Preventing Transmission of Infectious Agents in Healthcare Settings."** — The operational document that codifies droplets as >5 μm / droplet nuclei (airborne) as <5 μm, plus the ~3 ft historical, later 1–2 m/6 ft distancing convention, for modern hospital practice. *(Confidence: high.)*
- Xie X, Li Y, Chwang ATY, Ho PL, Seto WH. **"How far droplets can move in indoor environments — revisiting the Wells evaporation–falling curve."** *Indoor Air*, 2007, 17(3):211–225. — Modern re-derivation of Wells' curve with real airflow/humidity; the key pre-2015 quantitative source for actual travel distance. *(Confidence: high.)*
- **Randall K, Ewing ET, Marr LC, Jimenez JL, Bourouiba L. "How did we get here: what are droplets and aerosols and how far do they go? A historical perspective on the transmission of respiratory infectious diseases."** *Interface Focus*, 2021, 11(6):20210049. doi:10.1098/rsfs.2021.0049. — **This is the definitive modern treatment of your exact question.** It's the paper that traced the citation trail above, showed Wells' 1934 papers never mention 5 μm, showed the real Wells cutoff is ~100 μm, and identified Langmuir's 1951/1964 conflation as the origin of the guideline figure. *(Confidence: high — read directly, not secondhand.)*
- Bourouiba L. **"Turbulent Gas Clouds and Respiratory Pathogen Emissions: Potential Implications for Reducing Transmission of COVID-19."** *JAMA*, 2020, 323(18):1837–1838. doi:10.1001/jama.2020.4756. — Shows droplets travel inside a gas cloud up to ~7–8 m, undermining the fixed-distance droplet/airborne dichotomy. *(Confidence: high.)*
- Tang JW, Marr LC, Li Y, Dancer SJ. **"Covid-19 has redefined airborne transmission."** *BMJ*, 2021, 373:n913. *(Confidence: medium-high — title/venue/authors confirmed, content summarized from search, not fully read.)*
- Wang CC, Prather KA, Sznitman J, Jimenez JL, Lakdawala SS, Tufekci Z, Marr LC. **"Airborne transmission of respiratory viruses."** *Science*, 2021, 373(6558):eabd9149. — Broad consensus review reframing droplet/aerosol as a continuum rather than a dichotomy. *(Confidence: medium — confirmed venue/authors/year via search, not independently read.)*

**Bottom line:** the "5 μm, 1–2 m" rule you describe is a real, widely codified infection-control convention (CDC/WHO, "Droplet Precautions" vs. "Airborne Precautions"), but it is not itself a validated scientific finding about transmission distance — it's a historical conflation of an unrelated 1930s–40s lung-deposition threshold with Wells' 1934 droplet-physics work, entrenched by Langmuir in 1951/1964 and only rigorously traced and challenged starting with Xie et al. (2007) and definitively with Randall, Ewing, Marr, Jimenez & Bourouiba (2021).
