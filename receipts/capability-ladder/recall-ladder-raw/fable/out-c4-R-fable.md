## Identification

**1. What this is called.** The core of this procedure — asking each rater to endorse *every* defensible answer rather than forcing a single choice, then pooling those endorsement sets and selecting one winner by a fixed rule — is what established fields call **approval voting** (the ballot itself is an "approval ballot," and the elicitation format is also called **set-valued** or **subset elicitation**). The elicitation half has its own older name in testing theory: **subset-selection testing** (also "free-choice" or "liberal" multiple-choice), with the mirror-image variant called **elimination testing**. The rationale given in your description — that a forced single choice throws away the rater's *partial knowledge*, while the listed set preserves it — is exactly the stated motivation in both the psychometric and the crowdsourcing literatures; the crowdsourcing instantiation is explicitly titled "approval voting." The comparative framing (pooled group vs. best individual member) belongs to the **wisdom-of-crowds / judgment-aggregation** literature. I did not find a single modern paper that owns the exact micro-protocol you describe (fixed line format, one paraphrased candidate per line, "don't pad," union across raters) — that reads like a bespoke annotation/LLM-judging harness — but every load-bearing component maps onto approval balloting over partial knowledge. Confidence: **high** on "approval voting / subset elicitation" as the standard name for the mechanism; **moderate** that no more specific established term exists for the full pipeline.

**2. Which fields own it.** Three, in historical order: **psychometrics / educational measurement** (subset-selection and elimination scoring of multiple-choice tests, 1950s); **social choice theory**, spanning political science and economics (approval voting proper, 1970s–80s, later absorbed into computational social choice in CS); and **crowdsourcing / human computation in machine learning** (approval voting as a label-elicitation and aggregation interface, 2015). Confidence: **high**.

**3. Oldest and most canonical treatments** (all verified by search during this session):

- **Dressel, P. L. & Schmid, J. (1953), "Some Modifications of the Multiple-Choice Item," *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 13(4): 574–595** — the oldest source I found; introduces the "free-choice" format where the test-taker marks any subset believed to contain the correct answer, explicitly to credit partial knowledge. Confidence: high (title/venue verified on [Sage Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001316445301300404); the page range 574–595 comes from secondary citations and I'd rate it very likely but not eyeballed on the article itself).
- **Coombs, C. H., Milholland, J. E. & Womer, F. B. (1956), "The Assessment of Partial Knowledge," *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 16: 13–37** — the canonical elimination-testing paper. Confidence: moderately high (details confirmed only via secondary citations, e.g. [this ERIC document](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505719.pdf)).
- **Brams, S. J. & Fishburn, P. C. (1978), "Approval Voting," *American Political Science Review* 72(3): 831–847** — the founding paper of approval voting as a social-choice mechanism; verified on [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/approval-voting/7CE5DEEE235794B0B12F76ADAE621482). Confidence: high.
- **Brams, S. J. & Fishburn, P. C. (1983), *Approval Voting*, Birkhäuser, Boston (xix + 198 pp.)** — the canonical book-length treatment; verified via [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:matsoc:v:6:y:1983:i:1:p:123-124) and the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/approvalvoting00bram). Confidence: high.
- **Shah, N. B., Zhou, D. & Peres, Y. (2015), "Approval Voting and Incentives in Crowdsourcing," ICML 2015, PMLR 37: 10–19** ([proceedings page](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/shaha15.html), [arXiv:1502.05696](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.05696)) — the bridge to modern rater aggregation. Its abstract matches your description almost verbatim: the single-choice interface "does not allow workers to convey their knowledge accurately," so they introduce "approval voting to utilize the expertise of workers who have partial knowledge of the true answer." Confidence: high.

**4. What I'm hedging on.** (a) The specific empirical claim in your description — union-pooled approval sets plus a fixed selection rule *matching or beating the best individual rater* while plurality voting on committed answers falls well short — is a wisdom-of-crowds-style result whose general form is standard (see e.g. the [Condorcet-style analysis "When is a crowd wise?"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1406.7563) and experimental comparisons of [approval vs. plurality accuracy](https://www.promarket.org/2023/04/21/different-voting-methods-produce-different-and-more-accurate-results/)), but I could not locate a canonical paper proving that exact comparative statement for this exact pipeline; I'd treat it as the describer's own finding rather than a named theorem. (b) The "listed readings with labels" phrasing smells like word-sense/ambiguity annotation, where multi-label elicitation is also established (e.g. [Passonneau et al., "Multiplicity and word sense," *Language Resources and Evaluation*, 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10579-012-9188-x)), but that literature is a consumer of the idea, not its owner. (c) An adjacent-but-distinct ML concept worth not confusing with this: "learning from partial/candidate labels" (superset learning), which trains classifiers on candidate sets rather than aggregating raters.
