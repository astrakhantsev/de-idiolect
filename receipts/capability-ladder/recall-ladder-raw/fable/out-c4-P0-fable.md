## Identification

**1. Standard name(s).** The claim maps onto a well-established distinction in the theory of **judgment aggregation**: the **premise-based procedure vs. the conclusion-based procedure** (in the original jurisprudence vocabulary, **issue-by-issue vs. case-by-case voting**). "Read the enumerations" = aggregate or interpret the judges' itemized judgments on the individual issues/premises; "not the votes" = don't just tally their bottom-line verdicts. The formal result showing these two can disagree is the **doctrinal paradox**, generalized by philosophers as the **discursive dilemma**. A second, independent embodiment of the same claim in measurement science is **analytic (component/rubric-based) scoring vs. holistic scoring** of rater panels: the finding that a single holistic score per rater is noisy and idiosyncratic, while the enumerated component judgments carry the recoverable signal.

**2. Fields that own it.** Primarily social choice theory and formal political philosophy (judgment aggregation), with roots in jurisprudence / law-and-economics (collegial court decision-making). The analytic-vs-holistic version belongs to educational measurement / psychometrics (writing and performance assessment). The idea has a recent, less canonical echo in NLP evaluation, where LLM-as-a-judge practice favors reading judge rationales/critiques over trusting scalar scores.

**3. Oldest and most canonical treatments** (all verified against sources I fetched):

- **Kornhauser, L. A. & Sager, L. G. (1986), "Unpacking the Court," *Yale Law Journal* 96: 82–117** — the original example of a three-judge court where issue-by-issue and case-by-case voting yield opposite verdicts ([Yale repository record](https://openyls.law.yale.edu/handle/20.500.13051/16441)).
- **Kornhauser, L. A. (1992), "Modeling Collegial Courts II: Legal Doctrine," *Journal of Law, Economics & Organization* 8: 441–470** — where the name "doctrinal paradox" was coined, per [Christian List's judgment-aggregation survey pages](https://personal.lse.ac.uk/list/JA/paradoxes-sub1.htm).
- **Kornhauser, L. A. & Sager, L. G. (1993), "The One and the Many: Adjudication in Collegial Courts," *California Law Review* 81: 1–59** — the fullest jurisprudential development.
- **Pettit, P. (2001), "Deliberative Democracy and the Discursive Dilemma," *Philosophical Issues* 11: 268–295** — the generalization beyond legal doctrine ([Wiley record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1758-2237.2001.tb00047.x)).
- **List, C. & Pettit, P. (2002), "Aggregating Sets of Judgments: An Impossibility Result," *Economics and Philosophy* 18: 89–110** — the founding formal theorem of judgment-aggregation theory ([Cambridge Core record](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/economics-and-philosophy/article/abs/aggregating-sets-of-judgments-an-impossibility-result/35BB2A979DC8D2548B3040A1757B058B); [LSE eprint](https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/704/1/List_Econ&Phil_18(1).pdf)).
- For the measurement-science version: **Diederich, P. B., French, J. W. & Carlton, S. T. (1961), "Factors in Judgments of Writing Ability," ETS Research Bulletin Series, Educational Testing Service** ([Wiley record](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.2333-8504.1961.tb00286.x); [ERIC ED002172](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED002172)) — 53 raters on 300 essays; over half the variance in holistic grades was error or idiosyncrasy, which is the founding demonstration that panel verdicts alone are poor data and motivated analytic scoring. Often cited as the birth of modern writing assessment.

**4. Confidence.**

- That premise-based vs. conclusion-based aggregation is the closest established formal concept: **high**. The mapping is almost word-for-word — Kornhauser & Sager's "issue-by-issue voting" is literally "read the enumerated issues, not the case votes."
- That this is the *single* intended referent: **moderate, hedged**. The note is one sentence, and there is a genuine second candidate: if the project's claim is empirical ("the enumerated findings are more informative/reliable than the scores") rather than procedural ("aggregate at the premise level"), the analytic-vs-holistic-scoring literature (Diederich 1961 onward) is the better home. I'd say the concept sits at the intersection of both, and both are pre-2015 and canonical.
- All citations above: **high** — each was confirmed via publisher, repository, or List's survey pages during this search, including volume/page details. One small hedge: I did not confirm the exact ETS report number for Diederich et al. (search results gave conflicting bulletin numbering), so I cite it by series and year only.
- The LLM-as-a-judge echo (rationales over scores, e.g. discussion around G-Eval and judge-panel practice): **moderate** — it is current professional practice with supporting blog/paper commentary ([DeepEval overview](https://deepeval.com/blog/llm-as-a-judge)), but I found no single canonical academic source stating the claim in this form, so I would not present it as the "established" owner.
