**Bottom line: the core phenomenon is not new.** The claim that a per-case-estimated multiplier must clear an accuracy bar before it beats a fixed default — because estimation noise manufactures overconfidence and past a break-even error level the estimate is *worse* than the default — is independently established in at least four literatures, with canonical treatments going back to 1961. What I could *not* find published anywhere is your specific quantitative schedule (RMSE-of-log-multiplier ≈0.3 → ~85% of gain, ≈0.6 → ~half, >1.0 → worse than default) for an extremization-type coefficient, so the parametrization may be yours — but it is a quantitative instantiation of a known phenomenon, not a new phenomenon, and I'd write it up that way.

Translating your vocabulary first: your "multiplier that scales the pooled evidence lean" is what the forecasting literature calls an **extremization / recalibration coefficient** (a linear coefficient on log-odds), and your "operating requirement" is what several fields treat as **estimation error in adaptive weights vs. a fixed/naive default**.

## Prior art, by field

**1. Forecasting/econometrics — the "forecast combination puzzle."** The finding that estimated "optimal" combination weights lose to fixed equal weights out of sample, precisely because of finite-sample error in estimating the weights. Oldest root: [Bates & Granger (1969), "The Combination of Forecasts"](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/GeomComb/html/comb_BG.html), which introduced estimated optimal weights; [Clemen (1989)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/intfor/v5y1989i4p559-583.html) reviewed the accumulated evidence that equal weights win in practice. The canonical formal explanation is [Smith & Wallis (2009), "A Simple Explanation of the Forecast Combination Puzzle," *Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics* 71(3):331–355](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/obuest/v71y2009i3p331-355.html) — I fetched and read the abstract: "The explanation lies in the effect of finite-sample error in estimating the combining weights." A fuller theory is [Claeskens, Magnus, Vasnev & Wang (2016), *IJF*](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169207016000327). **Confidence: high** — citations verified against the RePEc mirror (Wiley/OUP pages were Cloudflare-blocked).

**2. Psychology/decision science — "unit weighting" / "improper linear models."** The oldest treatment of your exact tradeoff: [Einhorn & Hogarth (1975), "Unit weighting schemes for decision making," *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* 9:171–192](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0030507375900446) analyzes, as a function of sample size and number of predictors, when regression-estimated weights beat fixed unit weights — noting unit weights are "'estimated' without error." [Dawes (1979), "The Robust Beauty of Improper Linear Models in Decision Making," *American Psychologist* 34:571–582](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/dawes/the-robust-beauty-of-improper-linear-models-in-decision-making.pdf) is the most famous statement. **Confidence: high.**

**3. Statistics — shrinkage.** The deepest root: [Stein (1956) and James & Stein (1961), "Estimation with Quadratic Loss," 4th Berkeley Symposium](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4612-0919-5_29) — noisy unbiased per-case estimates are dominated by shrinking toward a fixed default; your ">1.0 error means use the default" is the boundary case of the shrinkage continuum. A modern cautionary echo in clinical prediction: [Van Calster et al. (2020)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0962280220921415) found estimated shrinkage "often worked poorly in individual datasets, in particular when it was most needed." **Confidence: high on the citations; the mapping to your framework is conceptual rather than literal.**

**4. Finance — "estimation risk" and the 1/N result.** This is the closest *structural* analog of your operating requirement: an explicit break-even data/accuracy requirement before estimated parameters beat a naive assumed default. [Jobson & Korkie (1980), "Estimation for Markowitz Efficient Portfolios," *JASA* 75:544–554](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01621459.1980.10477507) is the pre-2015 anchor; [DeMiguel, Garlappi & Uppal (2009), "Optimal Versus Naive Diversification," *RFS* 22(5):1915–1953](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v22y2009i5p1915-1953.html) (abstract verified) computes the exact analog of your schedule: ~3,000 months of history needed for 25 assets, ~6,000 for 50, before estimated weights beat the *assumed, unfitted* 1/N default — note that 1/N is an assumed default, so even your cold-start twist (fallback is assumed, not fitted) has a direct analog here. Klein & Bawa (1976) is often cited as the origin of "estimation risk," but my searches only confirmed the related Bawa, Brown & Klein (1979) volume — **treat that specific citation as unverified; the rest is high confidence.**

**5. Your multiplier itself — "extremizing" aggregated forecasts.** The structure (fixed vs. tuned coefficient scaling pooled log-odds evidence) is standard in the forecast-aggregation literature: [Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone & Ungar (2014), "Two Reasons to Make Aggregated Probability Forecasts More Extreme," *Decision Analysis* 11(2):133–145](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/deca.2014.0293) and [Satopää et al. (2014), *IJF* 30(2):344–356](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/biL94PKfeHmgHY6qe/principled-extremizing-of-aggregated-forecasts) (both pre-2015); when per-situation tuning of the coefficient helps or hurts is treated in [Lichtendahl et al., "Extremizing and Antiextremizing in Bayesian Ensembles of Binary-Event Forecasts," *Operations Research* (2022)](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/opre.2021.2176). **Confidence: high.**

**6. The closest single match to your cold-start operating requirement:** [Siegert, Sansom & Williams (2016), "Parameter uncertainty in forecast recalibration," *QJRMS* (arXiv:1509.07102)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.07102) — abstract read directly: recalibration coefficients fitted on short archives are themselves uncertain, this "artefact is usually ignored," and accounting for it improves skill and reliability. Meteorology owns this one (the Model Output Statistics recalibration tradition). It frames the problem as parameter uncertainty to be integrated over, not as an error-tolerance schedule — that framing difference is real but modest. **Confidence: high that this is the nearest neighbor; medium that nothing closer exists in the MOS literature I didn't reach.**

**7. Your "noise manufactures overconfidence" mechanism:** [Erev, Wallsten & Budescu (1994), "Simultaneous over- and underconfidence: The role of error in judgment processes," *Psychological Review* 101(3):519–527](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037%2F0033-295X.101.3.519) — random error in probability judgments produces artifactual overconfidence. Pre-2015 and canonical (with a known [critique by Brenner (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11089412/), so cite it as contested in degree, not in mechanism). **Confidence: high.**

**8. The "cold-start" name** belongs to recommender systems — [Schein, Popescul, Ungar & Pennock (2002), "Methods and Metrics for Cold-Start Recommendations," SIGIR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_start_(recommender_systems)). Borrowing the term is fine; just know it carries that field's association. **Confidence: high on the term's ownership.**

## What may still be yours — with explicit hedges

I found no publication that (a) characterizes tolerable estimator error as a *schedule* keyed to RMSE of the log coefficient, (b) derives it estimator-independently by injecting synthetic error, and (c) locates the break-even against an *assumed* rather than fitted default, all for an extremization-type multiplier. So the specific curve and the cold-start framing of the benchmark plausibly survive as a contribution. Hedges: absence of evidence here is weak — this exact question could live inside the meteorological MOS/recalibration literature or the post-2014 IARPA-tournament aggregation literature under phrasing my queries missed, and the synthetic-error-injection method itself is standard sensitivity-analysis practice. Also note DeMiguel et al. (2009) already did the "break-even requirement against an assumed default" computation in their domain, so the *move* is precedented even if your curve is not.

My recommendation for the write-up: present the schedule as a quantitative operating characteristic for extremization coefficients in your audit setting, explicitly situated in this lineage (Einhorn & Hogarth 1975, James & Stein 1961, Smith & Wallis 2009, DeMiguel et al. 2009, Siegert et al. 2016, Erev et al. 1994) — not as a new phenomenon. Given your FLF experience with Bröcker (2008), the highest-risk unswept corner is the meteorological verification literature; if you want, I can do a targeted sweep of the MOS/skill-score journals (QJRMS, *Monthly Weather Review*, *Weather and Forecasting*) for an error-tolerance-schedule equivalent before you commit to the novelty framing.
