**(A) FROM KNOWLEDGE, BEFORE SEARCHING**

- The vocabulary problem — information retrieval / HCI — Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais, CACM 1987 — the foundational measurement that two people spontaneously choose the same term for the same thing less than 20% of the time; the direct ancestor of every "your search terms aren't the author's terms" argument — no quote, recalled — **RECALLED**
- Controlled vocabularies and syndetic cross-reference structure — library science — Cutter's *Rules for a Printed Dictionary Catalogue* (1876), Library of Congress Subject Headings (c. 1898), MeSH (National Library of Medicine, 1960, descending from Billings' *Index Medicus*, 1879) — a human indexer assigns a canonical term regardless of the author's wording, and "see" references route every synonym to it; this is the original machinery against exactly this failure — **RECALLED**
- Roget's *Thesaurus* (1852) — lexicography — Peter Mark Roget — organized by concept rather than alphabet, i.e. an idea→all-its-wordings index; the inverse of a dictionary — **RECALLED**
- Query expansion, relevance feedback (Rocchio 1971), pseudo-relevance feedback, WordNet (Miller, Princeton, 1985–) — IR/NLP — compensate for mismatch by adding terms rather than escaping terms — **RECALLED**
- Latent Semantic Indexing/Analysis — IR — Deerwester, Dumais, Furnas, Landauer, Harshman, JASIS 1990; Bellcore patent 4,839,853 — indexes on latent concept dimensions from SVD of a term-document matrix, explicitly built to defeat synonymy — **RECALLED**
- Dense/neural retrieval for machine searchers — IR — Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych 2019), DPR (Karpukhin et al. 2020), ColBERT (Khattab & Zaharia 2020), SPLADE, doc2query/docTTTTTquery (Nogueira & Lin 2019), HyDE (Gao, Ma, Lin, Callan 2022) — HyDE generates a hypothetical answer document and embeds *that*, sidestepping query-document vocabulary mismatch — **RECALLED**
- Evaluation campaigns — IR — TREC (NIST, 1992–), CLEF, NTCIR; BEIR (Thakur et al. 2021), MTEB — measured recall/nDCG rather than claimed — **RECALLED**
- Citation indexing — bibliometrics/law — Garfield, *Science* 1955; Science Citation Index 1964; descending from Shepard's Citations (1873, legal); co-citation (Small 1973), bibliographic coupling (Kessler 1963) — indexes on who-cites-whom, not on words — **RECALLED**
- Structure- and sequence-based search — chemistry/molecular biology — CAS Registry, substructure and Markush search; BLAST (Altschul, Gish, Miller, Myers, Lipman 1990), preceded by Smith-Waterman (1981) and FASTA (1985) — index on the molecule or the sequence itself; wording is irrelevant — **RECALLED**
- Phonetic indexing — records/genealogy — Soundex, Russell patent 1918, used in US censuses — indexes on sound, not spelling — **RECALLED**
- Patent classification and examiner prior-art search — patent law — IPC (Strasbourg, 1971), CPC (EPO+USPTO, 2013) — examiners search classes, not just keywords, because novelty is legally the question — **RECALLED**
- Literature-based discovery — information science — Don Swanson, 1986 (fish oil/Raynaud's; migraine/magnesium), ABC model, "undiscovered public knowledge" — machinery for the case where the connection exists in public literature but no one's search reached it — **RECALLED**
- Systematic review search methodology — evidence synthesis — Cochrane, PRISMA, PRESS peer review of search strategies, sensitivity-maximizing filters, capture-recapture for search completeness — recall-oriented protocol built because a missed trial kills people — **RECALLED**
- The agreement problem — multiple fields — Condorcet's jury theorem (1785) requires independence; TREC pooling bias (Zobel 1998; Buckley, Dimmick, Soboroff, Voorhees 2007); ensemble diversity measures (Kuncheva & Whitaker 2003); algorithmic monoculture (Kleinberg & Raghavan, PNAS 2021); outcome homogenization (Bommasani et al., NeurIPS 2022) — **RECALLED**
- Fields I expected to matter that aren't obvious from the description: law (Shepard's), ecology (capture-recapture population estimation), intelligence analysis (Heuer's Analysis of Competing Hypotheses; groupthink; the WMD Commission on correlated analytic failure), trademark search (phonetic and Vienna figurative classification), record linkage (Fellegi–Sunter 1969), metrology (correlated systematic error) — **RECALLED**

**(B) AFTER VERIFICATION**

- Syndetic cross-reference structure, Rules 101–103 — library cataloguing — Charles Ammi Cutter, *Rules for a Dictionary Catalogue*, Third Edition (first ed. 1876, US Bureau of Education) — "Of two exactly synonymous names choose one and make a reference from the other." and, naming the failure mode itself: "there is no reason for increasing the evil by separating headings that are really synonymous, certainly not for dividing a subject in this way for verbal causes and giving no hint that it has been divided." Cutter also anticipates vocabulary drift over time: "I am told that medical nomenclature has changed largely three times within the present century. How is the cataloguer, unless he happens to be a medical man, to escape occasionally putting works on one disease under three different heads?" — https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59215/pg59215.txt — **PRIMARY-BODY** — *identifier conflict: the Gutenberg text is titled "Rules for a Dictionary Catalogue" and is labelled "Third Edition"; the 1876 first edition was titled "Rules for a Printed Dictionary Catalogue". I read Rules 101–103 in the Third Edition, not in the 1876 text.*
- Citation indexing — bibliometrics, descending from law — Eugene Garfield, "Citation Indexes for Science: A New Dimension in Documentation through Association of Ideas," *Science* Vol. 122, No. 3159, pp. 108–111, 15 July 1955 — Garfield states the vocabulary problem 32 years before Furnas and rejects terminology fixes outright: "even an ideal standardization of terminology and nomenclature will not solve the problem of subject analysis." His statement of the gap: "What seems to be needed, then, in addition to better and more comprehensive indexes, alphabetical and classified, are new types of bibliographic tools that can help to span the gap between the subject approach of those who create documents — that is, authors — and the subject approach of the scientist who seeks information." And what citation indexing buys: "By virtue of its different construction, it tends to bring together material that would never be collated by the usual subject indexing." Ancestry named in the body: "Since 1873 the legal profession has been provided with an invaluable research tool known as Shepard's Citations" — http://garfield.library.upenn.edu/papers/science_v122v3159p108y1955.html — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- Pooling, and the measured proof that many searchers sharing a bias produce false confidence — IR evaluation — Chris Buckley (Sabir Research), Darrin Dimmick, Ian Soboroff, Ellen Voorhees (NIST), "Bias and the Limits of Pooling for Large Collections," 2007 (*Information Retrieval*) — this is the closest thing to institutional machinery for the agreement problem, and it reports its own failure. The design: "In pooling, a set of documents to be judged for a topic (the 'pool') is constructed by taking the union of the top λ documents retrieved for the topic by a variety of different retrieval methods... documents not in the pool are assumed to be irrelevant to that topic." The load-bearing assumption: "The crucial assumption of pooling is that the sample of relevant documents found by judging just the pool is unbiased with respect to different retrieval approaches." The failure: "we show that pools created during the TREC 2005 workshop exhibit a specific bias in favor of relevant documents that contain topic title words. These documents are retrieved by systems that are behaving reasonably, in that they rank documents containing the topic words first. As the document set size grows, these documents fill the pool, squeezing out other kinds of relevant documents." And the sentence that answers the user's second failure mode most directly — agreement masking the error: "The terabyte collections' high titlestat rank values explain why the LOU test shows comparatively minor variations in scores: all of the pool runs were at least implicitly targeting title-word-containing documents and so match the bias in the judgments for the collection." Consequence: "a system based on the presence of semantic concepts instead of the presence of specific terms may retrieve a high proportion of unjudged documents which are relevant but which do not contain title words, and thus have a lower evaluation score than warranted." — https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=51236 — **PRIMARY-FULL**
- The numbers from that paper — IR evaluation — same source — 50 runs contributed to the AQUAINT pool; one run (sab05ror1) built its query from *relevance data* rather than the topic's wording and "405 of these 2750 were uniquely relevant, i.e., were relevant documents that only this run contributed to the pool... the ratio of number uniquely relevant to the number contributed to the pool is higher than any other run in TREC's history for the major ad hoc collections." Leave-one-out impact: "if sab05ror1 had not contributed to the pool, its MAP score would have been 0.202 instead of 0.266". The word-overlap statistic titlestat_rel was 0.588 for Disks4&5 vs 0.719 for AQUAINT on the same 50 topics, "greater for the AQUAINT collection than it is for the Disks4&5 collection for 48 of the 50 topics... (p = 6.25 · 10−10 according to a paired t-test)"; for the terabyte collections 0.889/0.898, meaning "any single title word occurred in nearly 9 out of 10 judged relevant documents on average." Crucially the diversity defense was tried and failed: "the diversity of the runs was expected to ensure a reliable sample of all types of relevant documents." — same URL — **PRIMARY-FULL**
- BEIR, and its independent rediscovery of the same bias in a modern neural benchmark — IR — Nandan Thakur, Nils Reimers, Andreas Rücklé, Abhishek Srivastava, Iryna Gurevych (UKP Lab, TU Darmstadt), NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks — "Many BEIR datasets are found to be subject to a lexical bias, i.e. a lexical based retrieval system like TF-IDF or BM25 has been used to retrieve the candidates for annotation... Such a lexical bias disfavours approaches that don't rely on lexical matching, like dense retrieval methods, as retrieved hits without lexical overlap are automatically assumed to be irrelevant, even though the hits might be relevant for a query." Measured on TREC-COVID (which *did* use pooling): Hole@10 was 6.4% for BM25 and 2.8% for docT5query but 30.6% for DPR and 31.8% for TAS-B. After manually filling 980 missing judgements, "for the dense retrieval system ANCE, the performance improves from 0.654 (slightly below BM25) to 0.735, which is 6.7 points above the BM25 performance", while docT5query moved "just from 0.713 to 0.714". This is Buckley et al.'s 2007 prediction coming true fourteen years later, and it means BEIR's headline result that "BM25 remains a strong baseline for zero-shot text retrieval" is partly an artifact of who built the benchmarks — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.08663v4 — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- Idea Novelty Checker — the most on-target system built for machine novelty-searchers — IR/NLP — Simra Shahid (Microsoft), Marissa Radensky, Raymond Fok (UW), Pao Siangliulue, Daniel S. Weld, Tom Hope (Allen Institute for AI), "Literature-Grounded Novelty Assessment of Scientific Ideas," arXiv:2506.22026, 27 June 2025 — it names the exact failure mode in shipped systems: of Sakana's AI Scientist, "it depends on keyword-based retrieval methods to get the most relevant papers to an idea, which may fail if the relevant papers do not contain the exact keywords," and independent evaluation "noted that the system can misclassify well-established concepts (micro-batching for stochastic gradient descent) as novel." Their Table 2, "Accuracy of predicting 'not novel'", is the single most on-target measurement I found: complete system 89.66%, relevance RankGPT 13.79%, embedding filtering 10.34%, snippet retrieval 8.62%, **keyword retrieval 5.17%** — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.22026 — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- The caveats on that number, from the body not the abstract — same source — the ablation used "58 ideas (comprising 13 'not novel' instances from our test set and 45 NLP papers from the literature)", so 5.17% is 3 of 58 and 89.66% is 52 of 58; the main test set is smaller still — "67 consensus-labeled examples (39 labeled as novel and 28 as non-novel)... (35 for training and 32 for testing)". Table 1 gives AI Scientist accuracy 0.47 and Cohen's Kappa **0.05** (chance-level agreement with experts), AI Researcher/GPT-4o 0.78 and Kappa 0.52, AI Researcher/Claude-3.5-Sonnet 0.56 and Kappa 0.19, best own system 0.81 and Kappa 0.59 — moderate agreement at best. The abstract's "approximately 13% higher agreement than existing approaches" is a *relative* Kappa gain (0.52→0.59) against one baseline only, and the comparison is prompts-only, not systems: "We compare only the prompts to assess novelty of these two approaches with ours, rather than the entire system." They also concede prompt fragility: "prompt 3 (accuracy = 0) and prompt 9 (accuracy = 0.6) include instructions to evaluate if the idea introduces unique methodologies... subtle variations in wording and instruction framing can significantly impact the classification." — same URL — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- An internal contradiction in that paper on the load-bearing point, reported rather than resolved — same source — Section 2 says of AI Scientist: "the decision of novelty evaluation relies on string matching for phrases like 'decision made: novel' or 'decision made: not novel.' If such a decision is not reached, the idea is automatically considered **novel**." Section 6.1 says: "It is important to note that AI Scientist defaults to **'not novel'** when it fails to reach a conclusion in novelty evaluation (18 out of 32 times), which may have impacted its agreement rates." Two search engines each quoted one of these at me and appeared to disagree; both were quoting the paper accurately — the paper contradicts itself. The direction of that default *is* the failure mode in question, and this paper states it both ways — same URL — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- Why every one of these benchmarks measures only one direction — same source — "we focus on the 'not novel' cases, since the ideas labeled novel in expert-labeled test data can vary with different retrieved paper sets." Novelty is falsifiable only by finding the thing; "absent" is not directly checkable, so the measurable quantity is always *recall of known-existing prior work*, never accuracy of a "new" verdict — same URL — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- Algorithmic monoculture — CS/game theory — Jon Kleinberg & Manish Raghavan, PNAS 118(22) e2018340118, 2021 (arXiv:2101.05853v2) — the canonical formal treatment, but narrower than its reputation and *not* a model of shared-query search agreement: "we show that algorithmic monoculture poses risks even in the absence of shocks." Its mechanism is competition for a scarce resource, formalized as a Braess' paradox in hiring: "for any θH, there exists θA > θH such that using the algorithmic ranking is a strictly dominant strategy for both firms, but social welfare would be higher if both firms used human evaluators." The sentence usually quoted for the correlated-failure idea is actually its framing of *others'* concerns: "a world in which everyone uses the same algorithm is susceptible to correlated failures when the algorithm finds itself in adverse conditions." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.05853v2 — **PRIMARY-FULL** — *the published PNAS version could not be retrieved (Cloudflare); I read the arXiv v2, which states it is the same paper.*
- Outcome homogenization, and a metric for lost independence — ML — Rishi Bommasani, Kathleen A. Creel, Dan Jurafsky, Ananya Kumar, Percy Liang (Stanford/Northeastern), NeurIPS 2022, arXiv:2211.13972 — this is the one that measures agreement-as-correlated-failure directly, and its own framing distinguishes it from Kleinberg & Raghavan: "their formalism considers harms experienced by decision-makers, whereas we center decision-subjects." The metric is the ratio of observed joint failure to the failure rate predicted under independence: "we measure individual-level outcome homogenization... by normalizing the observed rate of systemic failure by the expected rate of systemic failure." The finding that matters most here — aggregate accuracy cannot see lost independence: "by looking at accuracy alone, these settings are indistinguishable, whereas they differ considerably in terms of the number of observed systemic failures. Our measures correctly identify discrepancies in these settings, even though the underlying error rates are the same." What it needs in order to work: per-individual outcomes from every decision-maker, aligned item-by-item — and they note "we often lack individual-level information (e.g. due to privacy concerns)". They also confirm shared inputs drive it: "the use of the same training data leads to greater outcome homogenization than the use of different (but identically distributed) training data," while cautioning "data sharing alone does not fully characterize homogeneity." — https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13972 — **PRIMARY-BODY**
- Capture-recapture for search completeness — ecology → epidemiology → evidence synthesis — Poorolajal, Haghdoost, Mahmoodi, Majdzadeh, Nasseri-Moghaddam, Fotouhi, *J Res Med Sci* 15(2):107–115, 2010 — the sharpest cross-field answer, and it answers both the "how would you know it's absent" and the "how much independence was lost" questions with the same instrument: "Capture-recapture method, called the Petersen method, has a very long history and is widely used in ecology to estimate the unknown size of wild animals' population... However, capture-recapture method can principally be applied to any situation where there are two or even more incomplete lists." The independence requirement, and the direction of its failure — which is precisely the user's second failure mode: "A critical assumption of capture-recapture methods is the independence of the sources so that either positively or negatively dependent sources may cause either underestimation or overestimation of the pooled estimates respectively." Positively dependent searchers → *underestimate* of what's missing → false reassurance. And the mechanism for measuring the dependence rather than assuming it away: "log-linear model is able to handle dependence among sources and adjust for it by including interaction terms in the model" — which requires three or more lists with item-level overlap; two lists cannot separate dependence from population size — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3082794/ — **PRIMARY-BODY**

**Oldest machinery found:** Roget's *Thesaurus* (1852) is the oldest on-target machinery — an idea→all-its-wordings index — but I could not verify the 1852 text; Shepard's Citations (1873) is next, verified only through Garfield's body; the oldest I read directly in a primary is Cutter's Rule 101 (1876 first edition, read in the Third Edition), which names the failure mode as "dividing a subject in this way for verbal causes and giving no hint that it has been divided."

**Single most on-target thing built for machine searchers:** the Idea Novelty Checker (Shahid, Radensky, Fok, Siangliulue, Weld, Hope — Microsoft/UW/Allen Institute for AI, arXiv:2506.22026, June 2025), whose Table 2 measures the exact failure: retrieval by keywords derived from the idea's own wording correctly recognizes an already-published idea as "not novel" **5.17% of the time** (3 of 58), rising to 89.66% with facet-based reranking — small-N, and produced by the authors ablating their own system, but directionally the most on-point number in this literature.

**The gap — stated as not found in these searches, never as does not exist:** I looked for and did not find any benchmark, metric, or system that measures *correlated failure across multiple machine novelty-checkers given the same idea in the same wording* — the second failure mode has owners in adjacent settings (Bommasani et al.'s homogenization metric; log-linear capture-recapture interaction terms; TREC's pooling diversity requirement) but I found none of them applied to novelty checking, and no benchmark reports how many independent machine searchers agreed wrongly on the same idea. Also not found: any automation of PRESS's second-librarian search peer review; any capture-recapture estimate of what a machine novelty-checker missed; and any published measurement of whether ensembling novelty-checkers that share an embedding model or a Semantic Scholar backend buys real independence. Additionally could not retrieve: Furnas et al. 1987 full text, Zobel 1998, Swanson 1986, the published PNAS version of Kleinberg & Raghavan, Roget's 1852 preface, and the PRESS fulltext.
