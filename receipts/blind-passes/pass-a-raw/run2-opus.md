## (B) After verification

- **Idols of the Market-place** — philosophy — Bacon, *Novum Organum* (1620), Book I, Aphorism LIX (Spedding trans.) — "Now words, being commonly framed and applied according to the capacity of the vulgar, follow those lines of division which are most obvious to the vulgar understanding. And whenever an understanding of greater acuteness or a more diligent observation would alter those lines to suit the true divisions of nature, words stand in the way and resist the change." — https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Novum_Organum/Book_I_(Spedding) — **VERIFIED**. Aphorism LX adds the second class of word-idols: "names of things which exist, but yet confused and ill-defined, and hastily and irregularly derived from realities." This is a closer fit than I expected: Bacon's claim is not merely that words are vague, but that a community's naming *resists correction by better observation* — the group's vocabulary actively defends itself.
- **Undiscovered public knowledge / noninteractive literatures** — information science — Swanson (1986), *Library Quarterly* 56(2):103–118 — "Knowledge can be public, yet undiscovered, if independently created fragments are logically related but never retrieved, brought together, and interpreted." And, carrying the false-confidence claim directly: "The search process, like a scientific theory, can be criticized and improved, but can never be verified as capable of retrieving all information relevant to a problem or theory. This essential incompleteness of search and retrieval therefore makes possible, and plausible, the existence of undiscovered public knowledge." — http://www.sciencengines.com/NPHS/Documents/UndiscoveredPublicKnowledge.pdf — **VERIFIED** (read the PDF; OCR runs some words together, quotes normalized). Body text confirms the Raynaud/fish-oil case: "The two literatures appear to be remarkably isolated from one another with respect to common authors and mutual citations." Note: Swanson never uses the words *vocabulary*, *terminology*, *jargon*, or *specialization* anywhere in the paper — his mechanism is citation/retrieval isolation, not naming. That is a real limit on the fit.

- **Task 5 — adjacent but distinct, with the boundary stated:**
  - **Undiscovered public knowledge (Swanson)** — *combinatorial*: two complementary fragments (A→B, B→C) that nobody joined. Yours is *duplicative*: the finished finding already exists. Collapsing these is the single biggest risk in your list — they have opposite remedies and Swanson's mechanism is citation isolation, not naming.
  - **NIH syndrome** — they *found* the prior work and rejected it. Boundary: appraisal vs. retrieval.
  - **Multiple discovery** — near-simultaneous, driven by "cultural preparedness"; nobody searched badly and often nobody is wrong. Boundary: inevitability/simultaneity vs. avoidable decades-later miss.
  - **Stigler's law** — misattribution of credit for work everyone can find. Boundary: naming vs. finding.
  - **Sleeping beauties** — the work is findable and even cited-eventually; dormancy is about demand and conceptual readiness. Boundary: reception vs. reachability.
  - **Prematurity (Stent)** — the work can't be connected to canonical knowledge at all. Boundary: conceptual gap vs. lexical gap — a premature discovery stays lost under perfect search; yours was one synonym away.
  - **Incommensurability (Kuhn)** — claims terms *cannot* be translated across paradigms. Yours translate fine; nobody tried. Boundary: impossibility vs. omission. Kuhn is a much stronger and more contested claim — borrowing it inflates your situation.
  - **Jingle fallacy** — same name, different things. Yours is the inverse (jangle).
  - **Groupthink** — conformity pressure suppressing dissent. Yours needs no suppression: the searchers sincerely and independently agreed *because they shared a premise*. Boundary: social pressure vs. common-cause correlated inputs.
  - **Hidden profile / shared information bias** — the group collectively *holds* the missing information and fails to pool it. Yours: nobody in the group holds it. Boundary: inside vs. outside the group.
  - **Filter bubble / echo chamber** — exogenous filtering imposed on you. Yours is endogenous and self-inflicted by your own naming.
  - **Streetlight effect** — you know the keys are elsewhere and search where it's easy anyway. Yours: the group has no idea an "elsewhere" exists. Boundary: knowingly misdirected vs. unknowingly bounded.
  - **Absence of evidence ≠ evidence of absence (Altman & Bland 1995, BMJ 311:485)** — the inferential error committed *after* the clean search. It's one component of your compound, not the whole. https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~wdmccall/os512d/EvidAbs.html — **UNVERIFIED**.
  - **Construct proliferation** — the aggregate state of a literature. Yours is one group's epistemic event.
  - **Reinventing the wheel** — names the outcome, silent on mechanism.

- **Task 6 — where I'd look that you would not:** *reliability engineering*, for **common-cause failure** — the formal treatment of why N redundant channels give ~zero extra protection when they share an input, which is exactly your several-searchers-briefed-from-the-same-documents feature, and it comes with quantitative models (beta-factor, IEC 61508) that the social-science framings lack; *terminology science / ISO TC 37 and onomasiology*, which formalized "start from the concept, not the word" in 1931; *chemical information science, bioinformatics, and the OEIS*, as the only fields I know that structurally eliminated the problem rather than mitigating it; *rhetoric & composition* (Swales 1990, discourse community — specialized lexis as a *defining criterion* of a research community, i.e. your vocabulary isn't a bug of the group, it's constitutive of it); *anthropology* (ethnoscience, folk taxonomy, componential analysis); *archival/museum documentation*; *legal research* across jurisdictions; *cross-lingual information retrieval*; and *Wikipedia's redirect/merge apparatus* as the largest working synonym-collapser ever built.

**Oldest thing I found:** Bacon, *Novum Organum* (1620), Book I, Aphorism LIX — verified against primary text — which already contains the non-obvious half of your phenomenon: not that words are imprecise, but that a community's words "stand in the way and resist the change" when better observation would redraw the boundaries. **True home, if I had to pick one:** information science — specifically the Furnas–Swanson axis. It is the only field that holds all three pieces at once: that names are arbitrary and divergent (Furnas), that retrieval keyed on names is therefore incomplete (Swanson), and that the incompleteness is *unprovable* — "can never be verified as capable of retrieving all information relevant to a problem" — which is precisely why a clean null search yields high confidence rather than doubt. But I'd flag that this is a pick under duress: no field owns the compound you described, no field has named it, and the closest thing to a professional home is not a discipline but an occupation — research librarianship, which treats it as a craft problem rather than a research object.
