CONCEPT-1:
This quantity is a number computed from three inputs: a starting belief expressed as a probability, a summary measure of how strongly a body of evidence leans toward or away from a claim, and a decision threshold — a probability above which one agrees to call the matter settled rather than open. It applies to a setting in which several sources each supply an evidence "lean" on a log-odds scale, those leans are averaged into a single average lean D̄, and a final belief is formed by adding a scaled version of that average to the starting belief on the log-odds scale: logit(p̂) = logit(π₀) + m · D̄. Here m is a free multiplier that sharpens the result when m > 1 and softens it when m < 1. This quantity is the specific value of m at which the final belief exactly reaches the threshold, so that any larger multiplier declares the matter settled and any smaller one leaves it open. It has the closed form m* = (logit(t) − logit(π₀)) / D̄: a ratio whose numerator depends only on chosen conventions (starting belief and threshold) and whose denominator depends only on the observed evidence. It is a tipping point, not a count of anything, and it is defined only when D̄ ≠ 0 and has the same sign as the numerator.

CONCEPT-2:
This is a quantitative specification stating how accurate a measuring procedure must be before using its output is better than not using it at all. It applies wherever a pipeline needs a numeric setting — here, a multiplier that scales pooled evidence — and one may either estimate that setting separately for each case or fall back to a single fixed value used for every case. The specification's input is the error of the estimator, summarized as the root-mean-square error of the logarithm of the estimated quantity relative to its true value; its output is the fraction retained of the maximum achievable improvement in prediction accuracy, measured by a standard scoring rule for probability forecasts. The stated content is a schedule: error ≲ 0.3 retains roughly 85% of the available gain; ≈ 0.6 retains about half; beyond ≈ 1.0 the estimator is actively harmful, producing worse predictions than the fixed fallback because its noise manufactures unwarranted confidence. Two conditions matter. First, the benchmark must be named: a fixed value chosen by fitting to already-resolved cases is a strong competitor but is unavailable when no resolved outcomes exist, in which case the honest comparison is against a merely assumed value. Second, the schedule is established by injecting synthetic errors of a given size rather than by running any particular estimator, so it characterizes an error level, not a method.

CONCEPT-3:
This is a claim about where a loss of quality arises within a two-stage procedure. In such a procedure, a rater first generates candidate answers or interpretations for an item, then applies a gate — a rule deciding whether to commit to one candidate or to withhold and hedge. Observed performance is worse than some attainable benchmark, and one wants to know which stage is responsible. This claim asserts that the generation stage is not the culprit: the needed material is present in what the rater produces, and the shortfall lies downstream, in how that material is filtered or thresholded. Evidence for it takes the form of showing the same rater, on the same items, producing rich candidate sets (for example, multiple defensible readings on about three-quarters of ambiguous items) while its gate commits narrowly (hedging only about a third of them), so richness and strictness coexist within a single pass. The claim is stated with explicit limits. It is supported by frozen, pre-registered comparisons — for instance, a performance gap of at least 0.10 on a fixed metric, required to reproduce on a held-out split. Its scope is restricted to the gates actually tested: no fixed re-thresholding of the rater's stated confidences transferred out of sample, and no assertion is made about untested gate families or about quantities never manipulated.

CONCEPT-4:
This is a procedure for extracting an answer from raters by using everything they put forward, rather than only their final pick. Its input is, for each item, one or more raters operating under a contract that asks each to list every defensible candidate answer in a fixed line format — one line per candidate, with a short paraphrase — with an explicit instruction not to pad the list, and in some versions with no request for a single final choice at all, so that the list itself is the response. From each rater one forms a candidate set S(item): the deduplicated collection of labels attached to its listed readings if it listed several, or the single label it chose if it committed to one. These sets are then pooled across raters — typically by union — and a simple fixed rule, decided in advance, selects one label from the pooled set. The output is a single answer per item, produced at full coverage: every item receives a decision. The procedure's point is comparative: applied to the same raters and items, it can match or beat the accuracy of the best individual rater, whereas pooling only the raters' committed single answers by voting can fall well short of the best member. It applies wherever raters can be instructed to enumerate alternatives and their listed material is recorded.
