Three of the four map cleanly onto well-developed literatures that predate the project by decades; TERM-2 is the one where the gloss underdetermines the answer, and I'd want to resolve a fork before pointing you anywhere. Overall caution: I'm confident about the field-and-canonical-term layer, moderately confident about most citations, and I flag the specific ones I'd check before quoting.

## TERM-1: "m*" — the per-question critical count

**Canonical terms.** This is the *number of raters/replications needed per item to reach a target precision*, and it has at least three separate canonical framings depending on what "needs" means:

- If m* is set by a **reliability target**: the Spearman–Brown prophecy formula, and more generally the **decision study (D-study)** in **Generalizability Theory**, which exists precisely to answer "how many raters/occasions/items do I need for a target dependability coefficient."
- If m* is set by a **decision-error target** (stop auditing when you can call the question): **sequential analysis** / **SPRT**, and its psychometric descendant, **variable-length stopping rules in computerized adaptive testing** (stop when the standard error or the posterior classification probability crosses a bound).
- If m* varies per item because items differ in difficulty and you're allocating a fixed budget: **adaptive / selective repeated labeling** and **dynamic label allocation** in crowdsourcing.

**Fields.** Psychometrics and educational measurement; sequential statistics and optimal experimental design; human computation / crowdsourcing; behavioral decision research on judge panels; latterly LLM-as-judge eval methodology, which mostly rediscovers the first three.

**Oldest treatments I'd expect.** Spearman (1910) and Brown (1910), both in *British Journal of Psychology* vol. 3 — the prophecy formula, in adjacent papers. Wald (1945), "Sequential tests of statistical hypotheses," *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* 16(2), and the 1947 *Sequential Analysis* book. Cronbach, Gleser, Nanda & Rajaratnam (1972), *The Dependability of Behavioral Measurements* (Wiley) — the D-study source; Brennan's *Generalizability Theory* (Springer, 2001) is the modern handbook. Dawid & Skene (1979), "Maximum likelihood estimation of observer error-rates using the EM algorithm," *Applied Statistics* 28(1) — the ancestor of every judge-accuracy latent model. On panel size specifically, Hogarth (1978), "A note on aggregating opinions," *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* — the diminishing-returns result on how many judges are worth having; also Einhorn, Hogarth & Klempner (1977) in *Psychological Bulletin* and Ashton (1986) on "how many and which ones," though I'm least sure of those two's exact titles and page details. For the crowdsourcing line: Sheng, Provost & Ipeirotis (2008), "Get Another Label? Improving Data Quality and Data Mining Using Multiple, Noisy Labelers," KDD — this is the closest single match to "per-question critical count" and I'm confident about it; then Whitehill et al. (2009, NIPS, GLAD) for item difficulty, and Dai, Mausam & Weld's TurKontrol (AAAI 2010; *Artificial Intelligence* 2013) for decision-theoretic stopping.

**Confidence and first check.** High confidence that the concept is not novel. The thing I'd verify first is which of the three framings your m* actually is, because they give genuinely different formulas and different literatures — ask whether m* is defined against a *reliability/variance* target (→ G-theory D-study, and the Spearman–Brown answer is closed-form) or against a *decision-error* target (→ SPRT, and the answer is a stopping boundary, not a count). If it's the latter, "critical count" is arguably a misnomer for a random stopping time, and that's worth knowing.

## TERM-2: "the operating requirement" — cold-start operating requirement for the audit unit

This is the one I'd push back on before answering, because the gloss is compatible with two different literatures.

**Fork A — it's a specification of acceptable error rates.** Then the canonical term is the **operating characteristic (OC) curve** and its associated **producer's/consumer's risk** at stated quality levels (AQL/LTPD), from **acceptance sampling** and statistical quality control; the near-identical construct in accounting is the **audit risk model** with a *tolerable deviation rate* and an *acceptable risk of overreliance*, where "sampling unit" is already a defined term of art. Under this reading "operating requirement" ≈ the (α, β) constraints that pin down sample size and acceptance number. Fields: statistical quality control, audit sampling, survey sampling; the ML cousin is choosing an **operating point** on an ROC curve. Oldest: Dodge & Romig (1929), "A method of sampling inspection," *Bell System Technical Journal* 8(4); Neyman & Pearson (1933) for the risk framing; Cochran's *Sampling Techniques* (1953, Wiley) for sample-size machinery; in auditing, Stringer's work on statistical sampling at Haskins & Sells from the early 1960s and AICPA SAS No. 39, *Audit Sampling* (1981). I'm confident about Dodge & Romig and SAS No. 39's existence and roughly its date; less sure of Stringer's exact venue, which I believe was an ASA Business and Economic Statistics Section proceedings paper around 1963 — verify before citing.

**Fork B — it's about bootstrapping when you have no history on the judges.** Then "cold start" is doing the real work and the literature is **cold-start estimation of annotator quality without gold**, **gold/honeypot seeding**, and **sample size for estimating a proportion**. Oldest anchors: Wilson (1927, *JASA*) and Clopper–Pearson (1934, *Biometrika*) for interval width as a function of n; Dawid & Skene (1979) again for no-gold latent quality; Ipeirotis, Provost & Wang (2010), "Quality management on Amazon Mechanical Turk," HCOMP, for the practical gold-seeding version; Platanios, Blum & Mitchell (2014), "Estimating Accuracy from Unlabeled Data," UAI, for the fully unlabeled case. The term "cold start" itself traces to recommender systems — Schein, Popescul, Ungar & Pennock (2002), "Methods and metrics for cold-start recommendations," SIGIR — which is where the borrowing likely comes from.

**Confidence and first check.** Low-to-moderate on the mapping, high on both candidate literatures being the right ones once the fork is resolved. Verify first: what "requirement" ranges over — is it a constraint the audit unit must *satisfy* (Fork A: an OC/risk specification) or a resource the audit unit *needs before it works* (Fork B: minimum gold/calibration data)? Second, check whether "audit unit" is your name for the *sampling unit* in the audit-sampling sense; if so, the accounting literature has ~60 years of directly transferable machinery and terminology, and adopting its vocabulary would be cheap.

## TERM-3: "the signal, not the cut, is the bottleneck"

**Canonical terms.** The separation of **discriminability (sensitivity, d′) from the decision criterion (threshold, bias)** — this is the founding move of **signal detection theory**, and your slogan is close to a restatement of it. The ML/eval phrasing is **discrimination vs. calibration**, or **ROC-curve position vs. operating point**: the formal fact underneath is that any monotone recalibration leaves the ranking (and hence AUC) untouched, so tuning the cut cannot buy discrimination you don't have. In forecast verification the same decomposition appears as **resolution vs. reliability** in Murphy's partition of the Brier score. The deepest version of the claim is decision-theoretic: **Blackwell's comparison of experiments** — a less-informative signal cannot be rescued by any decision rule over it, which is essentially your bottleneck claim proved.

**Fields.** Psychophysics and signal detection theory; medical decision making and diagnostic testing; machine learning evaluation; meteorological forecast verification; statistical decision theory and information economics.

**Oldest treatments.** Peterson, Birdsall & Fox (1954), "The theory of signal detectability," *Transactions of the IRE Professional Group on Information Theory*; Tanner & Swets (1954), "A decision-making theory of visual detection," *Psychological Review* 61(6); Green & Swets (1966), *Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics* (Wiley) — the canonical book. Blackwell (1951), "Comparison of experiments," *Proceedings of the Second Berkeley Symposium*, and (1953), "Equivalent comparisons of experiments," *Annals of Mathematical Statistics* 24(2). Murphy (1973), "A new vector partition of the probability score," *Journal of Applied Meteorology* 12(4). Metz (1978), "Basic principles of ROC analysis," *Seminars in Nuclear Medicine*. Provost & Fawcett (2001), "Robust classification for imprecise environments," *Machine Learning* 42 — the ROC convex hull, which is the direct argument that threshold choice is a deferred, cost-dependent decision and not where accuracy lives. Swets, Dawes & Monahan (2000), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 1(1), argues the accuracy-vs-threshold separation for applied audiences about as explicitly as anyone has. On the calibration side: Platt (1999), Zadrozny & Elkan (2002, KDD), Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana (2005, ICML). Fawcett (2006), "An introduction to ROC analysis," *Pattern Recognition Letters* 27(8), is the standard tutorial.

**Confidence and first check.** High on the mapping — I'd say TERM-3 has no novel content relative to SDT, and the honest framing in a writeup is "we restate the SDT sensitivity/criterion distinction for judge credences." Verify first whether your claim is the *trivially true* version (monotone recalibration preserves ranking, so the cut can't add discrimination) or an *empirical* version (in our setting, realistic cut-tuning gains are small relative to signal gains). The first needs no evidence and no experiment; the second needs a measured ROC and is the only version worth defending.

## TERM-4: "read the enumerations, not the votes"

**Canonical terms.** Several literatures converge here, and which one you want depends on why the enumerations beat the votes:

- If it's because a scalar vote discards information the judge actually surfaced: **hidden profile / biased information sampling** in group decision making, and the general **pooling of unshared information** result.
- If it's because the union of independently enumerated items has better coverage than any tally: **nominal group technique** and the **pooling of non-redundant ideas** finding from brainstorming research; the applied versions are **capture–recapture defect estimation** in software inspection and usability evaluation, and **pooling** in IR test-collection construction (where you literally read every system's enumerated candidates rather than their scores).
- If it's because aggregating over the *premises* gives a different (and better-justified) result than aggregating over the *conclusion*: **judgment aggregation**, the **doctrinal paradox** / **discursive dilemma**, and the **premise-based vs. conclusion-based procedure** distinction. This is the sharpest formal match to "not the votes."
- Adjacent: the **Condorcet Jury Theorem** and its failure under correlated errors, which is the reason vote tallies over LLM judges are less informative than they look.

**Fields.** Social psychology of groups; decision analysis (Delphi and its relatives); social choice theory and analytic philosophy/law; information retrieval evaluation; software engineering and HCI evaluation.

**Oldest treatments.** Taylor, Berry & Block (1958), "Does group participation when using brainstorming facilitate or inhibit creative thinking?", *Administrative Science Quarterly* 3(1) — the original nominal-groups-beat-interacting-groups result. Dalkey & Helmer (1963), "An experimental application of the Delphi method to the use of experts," *Management Science* 9(3). Delbecq & Van de Ven (1971) in the *Journal of Applied Behavioral Science* for NGT proper — I'm fairly but not fully sure of the exact title and issue. Stasser & Titus (1985), "Pooling of unshared information in group decision making," *JPSP* 48(6) — the hidden-profile paradigm, and the single best pointer if your claim is "the vote hides what the judge knew." Kornhauser & Sager (1986), "Unpacking the Court," *Yale Law Journal*, for the doctrinal paradox, then List & Pettit (2002), "Aggregating sets of judgments: An impossibility result," *Economics and Philosophy* 18(1). Grofman, Owen & Feld (1983), "Thirteen theorems in search of the truth," *Theory and Decision*, for Condorcet under correlated judges. Nielsen & Landauer (1993), "A mathematical model of the finding of usability problems," INTERCHI '93, for the union-of-enumerations model. For IR pooling, the lineage runs from Spärck Jones & van Rijsbergen's mid-1970s ideal-test-collection report through the TREC pooling practice documented by Voorhees & Harman — I'd verify the 1975 report's exact title and report number before citing it.

**Confidence and first check.** Moderate-to-high on the field, moderate on which sub-literature is *your* claim, because "enumerations" is doing unexamined work. Verify first: are judges enumerating **reasons for a shared verdict** (→ hidden profile, Delphi, premise-based aggregation) or **discovered items whose union is the output** (→ nominal groups, capture–recapture, IR pooling)? Those have different aggregation math — the second lets you estimate what *no* judge found, which the vote-based framing can't even express, and that's probably the strongest form of your claim if it applies.

## Two cross-cutting notes

The four terms are not independent: TERM-1 and TERM-2 are the same sample-size problem viewed from the item side and the unit side, and in acceptance-sampling terms m* and the operating requirement are related by the OC curve — the requirement determines the count. If your project treats them as separate constructs, that's worth a second look; the classical framing derives one from the other rather than positing both.

And the general pattern here: all four are renamings. That's not a criticism of the research, but before publishing or circulating I'd retitle at least TERM-3 (it's the SDT sensitivity/criterion distinction) and probably TERM-1 (it's a D-study or an SPRT stopping rule) to the canonical vocabulary, since private coinages for century-old concepts cost you reviewers and make the prior art invisible to your own future search.
