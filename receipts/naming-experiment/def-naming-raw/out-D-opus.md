All four have established names; none of them look novel as concepts. The short version: **(1)** is a threshold/tipping-point value ("switching value") of an extremization coefficient, computed reverse-Bayes style; **(2)** is a break-even error tolerance for a plug-in estimator against a constant, expressed as a skill score against a named reference; **(3)** is a criterion-versus-sensitivity localization claim, i.e. selective prediction with a reject option where the threshold rather than the generator is blamed; **(4)** is set-level (rather than abstract-level) combination of classifiers, which in social choice is approval voting.

Detail below, with confidence and verification notes per item.

## CONCEPT-1 — critical/tipping-point value of the extremization coefficient

**Canonical terms.** The object is a *threshold value* (also *switching value*, *break-even value*, *tipping point*) of a free parameter in a one-way sensitivity analysis: the parameter value at which a decision flips. The construction — fix a target posterior, solve backwards for the parameter that produces it — is the *reverse-Bayes* move; Matthews' *critical prior* is the same algebra with π₀ as the unknown instead of λ. The surrounding machinery has its own names worth using: D̄ on a log-odds scale is *weight of evidence* (Good/Turing, measured in bans/decibans); `logit(p̂) = logit(π₀) + λ·D̄` is a *logarithmic opinion pool* with an *extremization coefficient*; λ is functionally an inverse temperature, the same knob as *temperature scaling* in calibration. λ\* itself is closest in spirit to the *E-value* family — "how strong would X have to be to change the conclusion" — and to Rosenbaum's Γ, the sensitivity parameter at which an observational conclusion tips.

**Fields.** Decision analysis and health economics (threshold analysis), Bayesian statistics (reverse-Bayes), epidemiology (sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding), judgmental forecasting/aggregation (extremization).

**Oldest treatments I'd expect.** Good, *Probability and the Weighing of Evidence* (Griffin, 1950) for the additive log-odds/weight-of-evidence form, and *Good Thinking* (Univ. of Minnesota Press, 1983) for the "device of imaginary results," which is the reverse-Bayes ancestor. Cornfield et al., "Smoking and lung cancer," *JNCI* 22 (1959) — the original how-much-would-it-take argument. Weinstein & Fineberg, *Clinical Decision Analysis* (Saunders, 1980) and Pauker & Kassirer, "The threshold approach to clinical decision making," *NEJM* 302 (1980) for threshold analysis proper. Rosenbaum, *Biometrika* 74 (1987), on sensitivity analysis in matched observational studies. Goodman, "Toward evidence-based medical statistics. 2: The Bayes factor," *Annals of Internal Medicine* 130 (1999), which does the "how much evidence to move this prior to that posterior" computation explicitly. For the aggregation side: Genest & Zidek, "Combining probability distributions," *Statistical Science* 1 (1986); Baron, Mellers, Tetlock, Stone & Ungar, "Two reasons to make aggregated probability forecasts more extreme," *Decision Analysis* 11(2) (2014); Satopää et al., "Combining multiple probability predictions using a simple logit model," *International Journal of Forecasting* 30(2) (2014). Matthews' reverse-Bayes paper is, I believe, in *Journal of Statistical Planning and Inference* around 2001 with published discussion — I'm confident the work exists and moderately confident about that venue and year.

**Confidence.** High on the concept mapping, high on Good, Cornfield, Pauker & Kassirer, Rosenbaum, Genest & Zidek, and Baron et al. Lower on Matthews' exact venue and on whether "switching value" (which I associate with World Bank cost-benefit practice) is a term your readers will recognize. **Verify first:** whether the forecasting literature has already named the critical extremization coefficient specifically — I don't believe it has, and if so your contribution is a naming, not a construction, which is worth being explicit about.

## CONCEPT-2 — break-even error tolerance (an accuracy requirement / error budget) for a plug-in estimator versus a constant

**Canonical terms.** The specification is a *break-even* or *error-tolerance* analysis; in engineering framing, an *error budget* or *accuracy requirement*. The underlying question — when does estimating a parameter per-case beat using one pooled constant — is the *bias–variance tradeoff* and, in its sharpest classical form, the *shrinkage / Stein* result: the separate-estimates-per-case estimator is inadmissible against shrinkage toward a common value precisely when noise dominates. "The estimator is actively harmful past RMSE ≈ 1.0" is that phenomenon, not a new one. Two of your stated conditions have canonical names you should probably borrow. The output measure — fraction retained of the maximum achievable improvement, in Brier units — is a *skill score* (specifically the Brier Skill Score), and your insistence that the benchmark be named is the standard, well-documented skill-score caveat: a skill score is uninterpretable without a stated reference forecast, and the fitted-constant-versus-assumed-constant distinction is exactly a choice of reference. The method of injecting synthetic errors to characterize an error level rather than a method is a *measurement-error / perturbation sensitivity analysis*; the classical root is attenuation from unreliability.

**Fields.** Statistical decision theory, forecast verification (meteorology), measurement-error statistics/psychometrics, and decision analysis (value of imperfect information).

**Oldest treatments I'd expect.** Brier, "Verification of forecasts expressed in terms of probability," *Monthly Weather Review* 78 (1950). Murphy, "A new vector partition of the probability score," *Journal of Applied Meteorology* 12 (1973), for the reliability/resolution/uncertainty decomposition; Murphy also has a mid-to-late-1980s *Monthly Weather Review* paper on the interpretation of skill scores that is the canonical "name your reference" cite — I'm confident such a paper exists, less confident of the exact year. James & Stein, "Estimation with quadratic loss," *Proc. 4th Berkeley Symposium* (1961), and Efron & Morris, "Data analysis using Stein's estimator and its generalizations," *JASA* 70 (1975). Raiffa & Schlaifer, *Applied Statistical Decision Theory* (Harvard Business School, 1961), and Howard, "Information value theory," *IEEE Trans. Systems Science and Cybernetics* (1966), for the value of imperfect information — the formal home of "how accurate must a signal be to be worth acting on." Spearman, "The proof and measurement of association between two things," *American Journal of Psychology* 15 (1904), for correction for attenuation. Copas, "Regression, prediction and shrinkage," *JRSS-B* 45 (1983). Vickers & Elkin, "Decision curve analysis," *Medical Decision Making* 26 (2006), for the modern "is this model worth using at all, versus a default" framing.

**Confidence.** High on the mapping and on Brier, Murphy 1973, James–Stein, Efron–Morris, Raiffa–Schlaifer, Howard, Spearman, Vickers–Elkin. Medium on the exact Murphy skill-score paper and on Copas's page numbers. **Verify first:** whether the specific numbers (0.3 → 85%, 0.6 → ~50%, 1.0 → break-even) are artifacts of your λ parameterization or robust across the reference choice — the break-even point should move when the benchmark moves from fitted-constant to assumed-constant, and if it doesn't, something is wrong with the simulation.

## CONCEPT-3 — the shortfall is in the criterion, not the sensitivity (a selection/readout-bottleneck claim)

**Canonical terms.** The two-stage structure — produce candidates, then commit or abstain — is *classification with a reject option* or *selective prediction* (the accuracy/coverage tradeoff). Your claim is that performance loss localizes to the reject rule rather than the generator. The oldest and cleanest vocabulary for that is signal detection theory: the deficit is in *criterion* (c), not *sensitivity* (d′). In the machine learning idiom the same split is *discrimination versus calibration* — the ranking is fine, the operating point is wrong. In NLP the evidentiary pattern you describe (rich candidate set, narrow commitment) is the classic *oracle gap* on an n-best list, which is exactly how reranking papers argue the bottleneck is downstream of generation. In psychology and linguistics the general claim has older forms: *competence versus performance*, and *availability versus accessibility* in memory. A directly relevant known result: Chow's reject rule is optimal only when posteriors are accurate, and with imperfect posterior estimates a single global threshold is suboptimal — which is essentially the mechanism your scope restriction is gesturing at.

**Fields.** Statistical pattern recognition, psychophysics/signal detection, NLP (reranking, calibration), and now LLM evaluation.

**Oldest treatments I'd expect.** Peterson, Birdsall & Fox, "The theory of signal detectability," *IRE Trans. Information Theory* (1954), and Tanner & Swets, "A decision-making theory of visual detection," *Psychological Review* 61 (1954); Green & Swets, *Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics* (Wiley, 1966). Chow, "An optimum character recognition system using decision functions," *IRE Trans. Electronic Computers* (1957), and "On optimum recognition error and reject tradeoff," *IEEE Trans. Information Theory* 16(1) (1970). Fumera, Roli & Giacinto, "Reject option with multiple thresholds," *Pattern Recognition* (2000) — I'm fairly confident of authors and title, less so of volume/pages. El-Yaniv & Wiener, "On the foundations of noise-free selective classification," *JMLR* 11 (2010). Collins, "Discriminative reranking for natural language parsing," *ICML* (2000), and Charniak & Johnson, *ACL* (2005), for the oracle-gap argument style. Chomsky, *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (MIT Press, 1965); Tulving & Pearlstone, "Availability versus accessibility of information in memory for words," *JVLVB* 5 (1966). For the ambiguity-has-multiple-defensible-readings premise: Aroyo & Welty, "Truth is a lie: crowd truth and the seven myths of human annotation," *AI Magazine* 36(1) (2015).

**Confidence.** High on the mapping and on Chow, Green & Swets, El-Yaniv & Wiener, Collins, Charniak & Johnson, Chomsky, Tulving & Pearlstone, Aroyo & Welty. Medium on Fumera et al.'s bibliographic details. **Verify first:** the SDT framing is not merely an analogy — if you can actually estimate d′ and c for the rater, the claim becomes testable rather than argued, and reviewers from psychophysics will ask why you didn't. Also verify that "no fixed re-thresholding transferred out of sample" isn't just an underpowered search over one threshold family; Chow-optimality failing under miscalibration predicts that class-conditional thresholds would transfer where a single global one doesn't.

## CONCEPT-4 — set-level (rather than abstract-level) combination; approval voting

**Canonical terms.** In multiple-classifier-systems vocabulary, this is combination at the *rank* or *set* level rather than the *abstract level* (single committed label) — the taxonomy is standard and old. The elicitation contract ("list every defensible candidate, don't pad") plus union-then-fixed-rule is, in social choice, *approval voting*: each voter names all acceptable alternatives instead of one, and the argument that it beats plurality is exactly Brams and Fishburn's. The general output type is *set-valued prediction* (the credal/imprecise-probability literature calls the classifiers *credal classifiers*; conformal prediction is the calibrated modern cousin, though it targets coverage guarantees rather than a fixed selection rule). In NLP terms, you are pooling n-best candidate sets across systems and applying a deterministic selector, at full coverage. The foil — majority vote over committed answers underperforming the best member — is a known failure of abstract-level combination under correlated errors.

**Fields.** Pattern recognition / ensemble methods, social choice theory, imprecise probability, NLP system combination.

**Oldest treatments I'd expect.** Xu, Krzyżak & Suen, "Methods of combining multiple classifiers and their applications to handwriting recognition," *IEEE Trans. Systems, Man, and Cybernetics* 22(3) (1992) — this is the source of the abstract/rank/measurement output-level taxonomy, and is the single citation I'd put first. Ho, Hull & Srihari, "Decision combination in multiple classifier systems," *IEEE TPAMI* 16(1) (1994) — rank-level combination that literally reduces candidate sets by union and intersection; this is the closest published ancestor of your procedure. Brams & Fishburn, "Approval voting," *American Political Science Review* 72(3) (1978), and their book *Approval Voting* (Birkhäuser, 1983). Further back: Borda (1781) and Condorcet's *Essai* (1785) for the general "richer ballots beat plurality" argument. Kuncheva, *Combining Pattern Classifiers* (Wiley, 2004), for the textbook treatment. Vovk, Gammerman & Shafer, *Algorithmic Learning in a Random World* (Springer, 2005), for set-valued prediction with coverage. Zaffalon's naive credal classifier work (*JSPI*, early 2000s) if you want the imprecise-probability lineage — I'm confident of the author and the idea, less of the exact citation.

**Confidence.** High on the mapping and on Xu et al. 1992, Ho et al. 1994, Brams & Fishburn 1978, Kuncheva 2004, Vovk et al. 2005. **Verify first:** read Ho, Hull & Srihari 1994 before writing this up — if their union-of-candidate-sets construction is as close as I remember, your procedure is a re-instantiation of it with LLMs as the base raters, and the honest framing is "known combination scheme, new rater class," not a new method. Second, check whether the beats-the-best-member result survives when the fixed selection rule is chosen on the same data it's evaluated on; a rule "decided in advance" still leaks if it was decided by looking at these items.

## Cross-cutting note

Concepts 1 and 2 share a parameter and a hazard worth stating once: λ is an extremization coefficient, and both the tipping point and the error tolerance are statements about how much the conclusion depends on a knob that has no ground truth. The most valuable literature check across all four is probably the forecast-aggregation line (Baron et al. 2014; Satopää et al. 2014; Genest & Zidek 1986), because it is where extremization, log-odds pooling, and the fitted-versus-assumed-constant question were all already in play before 2015 — and it's the field most likely to have a name for something you're currently naming yourself.

I've hedged every citation I'm not sure of, and I have not quoted any of them. The four I'd treat as most load-bearing and most worth confirming in a database before you rely on them are Matthews' reverse-Bayes venue, the Murphy skill-score paper, Fumera/Roli/Giacinto, and Ho/Hull/Srihari — the first three because I'm unsure of details, the last because it may substantially preempt CONCEPT-4.
