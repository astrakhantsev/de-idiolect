**TL;DR:** Part 1 is **automatic term extraction / terminology recognition** plus **novel word sense detection**; part 2 is **definition generation** under a **controlled defining vocabulary** (lexicography's "defining vocabulary," terminology science's "terminological definition," linguistics' **Natural Semantic Metalanguage** explications); part 3 is **ontology matching / vocabulary mapping** with SKOS-style typed correspondences. The whole system is an attack on the **"vocabulary problem"** (Furnas et al. 1987) in service of **literature-based discovery** (Swanson 1986), architected in the **"emergent semantics"** style (no global ontology). The assembly of parts 2+3 **was built in 2025** — the GenOM ontology matcher — but the full three-part pipeline with the closed-metalanguage constraint and the discourse-watching trigger has, as far as I could find, never been built as one thing.

## 1. Accepted names for the system and its parts

**The underlying problem** has two canonical names. In human–computer interaction and information retrieval it is the **vocabulary problem**: [Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais (1987)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/32206.32212) showed experimentally that two people choose the same name for the same thing with probability below 0.20, so single-keyword lookup fails 80–90% of the time. In information science, the consequence you describe — groups failing to find each other's relevant work — is **undiscovered public knowledge**, [Swanson's (1986)](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/403510/summary) term for logically connected literatures that "are 'noninteractive'" because their communities don't read or cite each other; the research program built on it is **literature-based discovery (LBD)**. (Philosophy of science has a third name for the phenomenon itself — Kuhnian **incommensurability** of specialist vocabularies — which I note from knowledge, unsearched.)

**Part 1 — noticing group-specific word use.** Two established tasks cover the two halves. Brand-new coinages: **automatic term extraction / automatic term recognition (ATE/ATR)**, the core task of **computational terminology**, plus **neologism detection** in corpus linguistics. Ordinary words with special local meanings: **novel (word) sense detection**, a branch of **word sense induction**, today generalized as **lexical semantic change detection**. The canonical modern statement is [Lau, Cook, McCarthy, Newman & Baldwin, "Word Sense Induction for Novel Sense Detection" (EACL 2012)](https://aclanthology.org/E12-1060/), which detects "words with emergent novel senses, as well as token occurrences of those senses" without a pre-existing sense inventory.

**Part 2 — self-contained explanations in a fixed small plain-word list.** This combines several named traditions. The automatic-writing part is **definition generation / definition modeling** in NLP ([Noraset et al., AAAI 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10996)). The "what kind of thing, inputs/outputs, when it applies" template is the **terminological definition** (intensional definition: superordinate concept + delimiting characteristics) standardized by ISO 704 in **terminology science**, descending from Aristotelian **genus–differentia** definition. The fixed-small-word-list constraint is, in lexicography, a **controlled defining vocabulary** (the Longman Defining Vocabulary is the famous one); in linguistic semantics it is exactly a **semantic explication** in the **Natural Semantic Metalanguage (NSM)**; and as an engineered language restriction it is a **controlled natural language (CNL)** (Kuhn's 2014 survey is the field's map). The [NSM homepage](https://nsm-approach.net/) states the doctrine in nearly your words: "there is a small core of basic, universal meanings, known as semantic primes… it allows us to describe the meanings of complex and culture-specific words and grammatical constructions (using semantic explications)… Explications and scripts are formulated in language that is clear, precise, cross-translatable, non-Anglocentric, and intelligible to people without specialist linguistic training." Your ban on names of people/methods/subjects matches both NSM practice and ISO 704's requirement that definitions be substitutable and self-contained.

**Part 3 — matching by meaning-descriptions, with typed pairwise verdicts.** This is **ontology matching / ontology alignment** (Semantic Web), the sibling of **schema matching** (databases) and of **vocabulary mapping / crosswalks between knowledge organization systems** (library & information science; also historically **"vocabulary switching"** and **"switching languages"**). Your four labels — same · more general · more specific · related — are precisely the standardized mapping-relation inventory. The [W3C SKOS Reference (2009), §10.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/) says: "The SKOS mapping properties are `skos:closeMatch`, `skos:exactMatch`, `skos:broadMatch`, `skos:narrowMatch` and `skos:relatedMatch`. These properties are used to state mapping (alignment) links between SKOS concepts in different concept schemes… The properties `skos:broadMatch` and `skos:narrowMatch` are used to state a hierarchical mapping link between two concepts. The property `skos:relatedMatch` is used to state an associative mapping link between two concepts." The same inventory (equivalence / hierarchical / associative mappings) is in [ISO 25964-2:2013](https://www.iso.org/standard/53658.html), and the same relations (≡, more general ⊒, less general ⊑) are what the [S-Match "semantic matching"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_matching) line computes.

**The design commitment** — pairwise, on-demand, no shared master list — is a named architectural position: **emergent semantics** ([Aberer et al., DASFAA 2004](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-30145-5_2) and the ["Emergent Semantics Systems" line](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-292/paper1.pdf)), which rejects "central components like global schemas or ontologies" in favor of decentralized local mappings; it is the explicit antithesis of the **UMLS Metathesaurus** approach (NLM, since 1986), which *is* the shared master list. Note one nuance: your fixed small defining word list is itself shared across groups — but it is a shared list of ordinary *words* (like NSM primes or the Longman list), not a shared registry of *concepts and their names*, so the commitment holds in the sense that matters.

## 3. Oldest treatments per part (each with pre-2015 anchors)

**Part 1.** Bourigault, "Surface grammatical analysis for the extraction of terminological noun phrases," COLING 1992 (LEXTER). Justeson & Katz, "Technical terminology: some linguistic properties and an algorithm for identification in running text," *Natural Language Engineering* 1(1), 1995. Kageura & Umino, "Methods of automatic term recognition: a review," *Terminology* 3(2), 1996 (year/volume from knowledge, lightly hedged). Frantzi, Ananiadou & Mima, C-value/NC-value, *Int. J. on Digital Libraries*, 2000. For the local-sense half: Erk, "Unknown word sense detection as outlier detection," HLT-NAACL 2006 (from knowledge, medium-high confidence), then [Lau et al. 2012](https://aclanthology.org/E12-1060/) (verified). Corpus monitoring of neologisms goes back to Renouf's AVIATOR project (early 1990s, Birmingham) — details hedged, not verified this session.

**Part 2.** The deep roots: Aristotle's genus–differentia (*Topics*); Wilkins, *An Essay towards a Real Character and a Philosophical Language*, 1668; Ogden, *Basic English*, 1930 (ancestral CNL). The first controlled defining vocabulary in practice: [West & Endicott, *The New Method English Dictionary*, 1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defining_vocabulary) — verified: "the first dictionary to use a defining vocabulary… written using a defining vocabulary of just 1,490 words" — followed by the *Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English*, 1978, with its ~2,000-word list. The primitive-meanings program: Wierzbicka, *Semantic Primitives*, Athenäum, 1972, then *Semantics: Primes and Universals*, OUP, 1996. Terminological definition-writing: Wüster's 1931 *Internationale Sprachnormung in der Technik* founding terminology science, codified in ISO 704. In AI, representing meaning in a small fixed primitive vocabulary so that same-meaning-different-words texts match: Schank, "Conceptual dependency: a theory of natural language understanding," *Cognitive Psychology* 3(4), 1972. The modern NLP task name arrives post-2015 with [Noraset et al. 2017](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/10996).

**Part 3.** Fellegi & Sunter, "A theory for record linkage," *JASA* 64, 1969 (matching by attributes rather than names). In information science proper, the oldest engineered treatment I verified is [Niehoff's Battelle work](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED247948.pdf): *Development of an Integrated Energy Vocabulary…*, Battelle Columbus Laboratories, 1976, which grew into the **Vocabulary Switching System** for "automated subject switching" across databases indexed with different thesauri (exact later citations hedged). Then: Sheth & Larson, *ACM Computing Surveys* 22(3), 1990 (semantic heterogeneity); Rahm & Bernstein, "A survey of approaches to automatic schema matching," *VLDB Journal* 10, 2001; Doan, Madhavan, Domingos & Halevy, GLUE, WWW 2002; [Giunchiglia, Shvaiko & Yatskevich, S-Match, ESWS 2004](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-74987-5_1) (typed relations: equivalence, more/less general); Euzenat & Shvaiko, *Ontology Matching*, Springer, 2007 (the field's textbook; OAEI benchmark campaigns run since ~2004); [SKOS, W3C Recommendation, 2009](https://www.w3.org/TR/skos-reference/); [ISO 25964-2:2013](https://www.niso.org/schemas/iso25964). For the whole-system motivation: [Furnas et al., *CACM* 30(11), 1987](https://cacm.acm.org/research/the-vocabulary-problem-in-human-system-communication/) and [Swanson, *Library Quarterly* 56(2) and *Perspectives in Biology and Medicine* 30(1), both 1986](https://philpapers.org/rec/SWAFOR); ARROWSMITH (Swanson & Smalheiser, *Artificial Intelligence* 91, 1997) is the first working discovery tool in this spirit.

## 4. Has the whole thing been built?

**Parts 2+3 as one system: yes, very recently.** [GenOM (Song, Chen & Schmidt, University of Manchester, 2025)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10703) is an LLM-based ontology matcher whose pipeline is literally "generate a definition, then match on the definitions instead of the labels." From the paper's introduction: "While ontologies serve as structured and machine-interpretable representations of conceptual knowledge, they are often engineered independently by different research communities or organisations. Each ontology typically reflects particular domain-specific objectives, modelling assumptions, and terminological conventions, which collectively result in substantial structural and semantic heterogeneity." And on the typed pairwise verdicts: "Through establishing formal relations such as equivalence, where two entities express the same or highly similar meanings, and subsumption, where one entity denotes a more general or more specific concept, OM serves as a cornerstone for semantic interoperability in distributed information environments." GenOM works pairwise between two given ontologies (consistent with your no-master-list commitment) and was evaluated on the OAEI Bio-ML track. Related LLM-era systems (LLM4OM, LogMap-LLM) exist in the same 2024–2025 wave.

**The cross-community discovery framing with typed same/parent/child links: yes, as an NLP task.** [SciCo (Cattan, Johnson, Weld, Dagan, Beltagy, Downey & Hope, AKBC 2021, Outstanding Paper)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08809) — "Hierarchical Cross-Document Coreference" for scientific concepts. From its body: concepts "often take diverse forms (e.g., *class-conditional image synthesis* and *categorical image generation*) or are ambiguous (e.g., *network architecture* in AI vs. systems research)," and the system infers "coreference clusters and a referential hierarchy between clusters." That is your part 3 applied to raw scientific text rather than curated ontologies — but it matches mentions in context directly; it does not go through generated definitions.

**The full three-part system as described: I found no single instance.** Specifically missing, on my evidence: (a) the **trigger** — watching a group's ongoing writing and firing definition-generation at the moment a term is detected as community-specific (ATE and novel-sense detection exist, definition generation exists, but I found no system wiring detection → on-demand definition → cross-community alignment); and (b) the **closed defining metalanguage** — no matching system I found constrains its generated definitions to a fixed small vocabulary with a ban on proper names and field names (GenOM's definitions are unrestricted natural language; NSM explications have that discipline but have never, to my knowledge, been generated automatically at scale for retrieval). What I would check first to confirm the negatives: the [OAEI / Ontology Matching workshop proceedings](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4144/om2025-LTpaper3.pdf) (ceur-ws.org, 2022–2026) for "definition generation" plus controlled-vocabulary constraints; the ACL Anthology for controllable/simplified definition generation applied to entity or concept linking; recent preprints on automating NSM explications with LLMs (I have a weak recollection such preprints exist, 2024–2025 — unverified); and follow-ups to SciCo from Tom Hope's group at AI2/HUJI, since their stated agenda is exactly cross-community concept bridging.
