A rule that takes an estimate's typical error size, on a smooth scale from zero upward, and outputs how much of a fixed decision-value prize the estimate keeps compared with using no estimate at all (the flat baseline that always gets some fixed default value). The share of prize kept declines gradually as the error size grows, so small errors cost a little and larger ones cost more. There is one particular error size where the share crosses below the no-estimate baseline; beyond that point the estimate is not just less useful but actively costs more than doing without it, because its wrongness gets mistaken for justified certainty, misleading whoever relies on it rather than merely giving them a noisier number. The threshold and the fractions of prize kept at given error sizes are fixed in advance, independent of any particular candidate, so a new estimate can be checked against them without needing to know how it was built or whether it will be judged first.
