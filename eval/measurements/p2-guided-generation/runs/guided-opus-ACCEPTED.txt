A stated rule that fixes how accurate a guessed number must be before using it helps more than skipping it entirely. The fixed comparison point is having no such guess at all — a plain fallback that carries no guess is the thing every candidate guess is measured against, not a flawless guess. The worth of a guess is scored on a sliding scale that falls off gradually as the gap between the guess and the truth grows, so a small gap keeps most of the benefit and a larger gap keeps less. There is a marked gap size past which the guess is no longer just weaker help but a net loss — worse than the no-guess fallback — because at that point it makes a decision look more settled than the evidence warrants, harming through false certainty rather than merely through being off by some amount. Because the rule names a specific allowed gap size, you can state and check it up front: measure any candidate's typical gap and compare it to the allowed size, without yet knowing whether that candidate will pass. It applies whenever someone must decide whether leaning on an uncertain guessed number is better than leaning on nothing at all.
