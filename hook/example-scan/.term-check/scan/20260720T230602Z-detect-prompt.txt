FLF's first-stated limitation of today's tools is that they "mostly produce single-user artifacts tuned to one investigator's context, not the kind that travel, combine, or survive (especially adversarial) scrutiny." We ask a narrower question beneath it: *mechanically, why doesn't knowledge combine across the people who hold it?*

[source: ENTRY.md]
- **Blair & Maron (1985, *CACM* 28(3):289–299.)** In a litigation-support corpus, lawyers "stipulated that they must be able to retrieve at least 75 percent of all the documents relevant to a given request"; measured recall was **about 20 percent**. The paper explains why the gap is invisible: "they have seen that the proportion of relevant documents in the retrieved set (i.e., Precision) is quite good (around 80 percent)." You can observe precision but not recall, so a clean search feels thorough even when it misses most of what exists.
- **Larsen & Bong (2016, *MIS Quarterly* 40(3):529–551.)** "The name of that construct can neither be trusted to elicit high recall … nor high precision." Doctoral students using full-text search retrieved **9%** of relevant constructs on average (per-task as low as **3%**); in the meta-analytic follow-up, "**83%** of participants would arrive at the conclusion that the relationship had not been tested at all." Extending Swanson (1986), they write that "the vast majority of *discovered* knowledge is hidden from the individual researcher by the very nature of the search process itself" and note that "the renaming of an existing construct (jangle fallacy) may increase the *perceived novelty* of a construct."
- **Furnas, Landauer, Gomez & Dumais (1987, *CACM* 30(11):964–971.)** The probability that two people spontaneously choose the same term for the same thing was **.07–.18**. Naming divergence is the default. (Their base rate is untrained people naming everyday objects; the magnitude transfers by analogy, not measurement.)
- The failure's name is old: the **jangle fallacy** — treating two things as different because they are named differently — is **Kelley (1927, pp. 63–64)**; its twin *jingle* is **Thorndike (1904) quoting Aikins**.

**From FLF's problem statement to this proposal in two steps.** Artifacts fail to travel, combine, or survive scrutiny because they are *indexed under private keys*: people search in the vocabulary they coined. [source: ENTRY.md]
- **(i) Split the definition's two roles.** Retrieval keys may be cheap, lossy, and plural; only verification requires a *faithful representation*, checked for round-trip fidelity. We propose a multi-key union (raw term + restated question + definition), but it remains conjectural: the first frozen fusion cell was null — no union beat the best single key, and fusion promoted the misroute document (§5.4) — while recall, false-positive load, and verification cost at scale remain unevaluated. Fidelity-before-retrieval remains the default ordering. Under the split, lossy keys may fetch *candidates*, but no mapping advances to verification or publication without a gate-passing representation.
- **(ii) The generator is replaceable behind a contract.** Any implementation — model, prompting strategy, extractive selection, or human — satisfies the architecture if its output passes the fidelity checklist (round-trip entailment + external-name ban) *and* the stage-2 lexical constraint. The contract is the design; no implementation, including this prototype's, has demonstrated full compliance. One step has been measured: regeneration guided by a checklist extracted from source excerpts cleared the fidelity half of the contract two-for-two where unguided generation failed (§5.4). Wordlist enforcement remains unbuilt.

**Two design commitments.**

**One shared artifact is allowed, and the distinction matters.** The fixed defining wordlist (LDOCE-style: small, domain-general, comparatively low-maintenance — revised only a few times in decades) *is* shared. We exclude an *authoritative, agreement-first domain concept resource* — a canonical ontology, termbase, or glossary on which communities must converge. The optional commons (commitment ii) remains permitted because it is derived, versioned, non-authoritative, and forkable. The invariant is "no canonical merge," not "nothing shared." Generic defining wordlists survived for decades largely because they require little curation, avoiding the economics blamed in §3's critiques for domain-resource failures. This is an argument, not a measurement.

- **Coin-time interception** (detection): when a term is minted, generate its neutral definition and propose the field's nearest existing terms, recording the translation while it is cheapest. §4.1's naming note applies this practice to the proposal's own term.
- **Claim-time estimand self-audit** (definition): regenerate the definition of what a claim *measures* and compare it with what the instrument *computes* — the error class that inverted Program A's headline.
- **Cite-time provenance check** (verification): before a load-bearing citation ships, open the primary, check the venue, and read past the ellipsis. This now has a measured yield and cost on the project's record *(measured)*. In a seeded sample of 100 model-proposed citation occurrences, roughly half to two-thirds survived verification depending on strictness (66/100 at the lenient primary; 43/91 at the strictest sensitivity, which also drops nine ineligible candidates; full contingency detail in the materials). Attrition came mostly from publisher access walls and attribution precision, at roughly one search and two fetches per work. Reachability constrained checking, arguing for durable verified mappings (the commons, §4.1) over repeated verification by each user.

- **The core pipeline** (generate definitions → embed → match → type relations) exists in prior work:
  - **SciCo-Radar** (arXiv:2409.15113, Forer & Hope): LLM-generated context-dependent, relational definitions for cross-document concept coreference with typed relations (mentions pre-annotated).

# The idiolect trap in the wild: a day-by-day, self-inflicted case study

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
The claim this document supports: groups (down to a single investigator working with LLM agents) coin private vocabulary for objects that established fields already own, run their novelty searches *in that vocabulary*, receive confident nulls, and read "not findable from here" as "unoccupied." The evidence here is not a constructed example but a case where it happened on the record — it happened to my own project, three times at the level of a headline novelty claim, and roughly twenty times at the level of individual renamings (not all of one class — the headline section gives the breakdown), over eighteen days of documented work, numbered below as Day 1 through Day 18.

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
## Background: what the research was trying to do

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
**Program A — the source-dependence detector (Days 1–15).** Research goal: when a verdict rests on many sources (news reports, studies, forecasters, LLM agents), the sources are usually correlated — copies, shared upstream origins, shared methods — so the *effective* number of independent looks is far smaller than the count. The program aimed to build a detector that estimates that effective number from the texts themselves and corrects overconfident aggregation. The central quantity was written `N_eff = N/(1+(N−1)ρ̄)`; the program coined terms like `m*` ("the audit unit" — how many independent looks a verdict needs) and "the operating requirement" (the accuracy an instrument must reach to be worth building).

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
Both programs practiced what most researchers would recognize as diligence: pre-registrations, adversarial review of every major digest, and repeated prior-art searches. That is what makes the case instructive — the trap fired *anyway*, because the searches were run in the programs' own coined vocabulary.

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
The project's conceptual vocabulary across both programs — `effective-N`, `m*`, "operating requirement," "decision-sensitivity," "gate," "credence," "coverage," "signal-not-cut," "read the enumerations," "set contract" — turned out, term by term, to be local renamings of objects owned by design-of-experiments, decision theory, meta-analysis, forecasting, psychometrics, argumentation, and proper-scoring-rule theory: roughly twenty renamings at the vocabulary level. **The twenty are not one class**, and counting them as one would repeat the inflation this document warns against. A post-hoc funnel over the full internal ledger sorts them into **~3 genuine recall misses** (dated coinage, a novelty search that failed to find the owner, the owner verified later), **~10 knowing borrowings** (the field's term was cited at or near coinage — context, not misses), **~4 self-errors** (the project misusing or confounding its own quantities), and **~4 rows whose owners remain asserted or defective** — that last class is not detailed in this document; it stays flagged in the project's novelty ledger. Three renamings reached the level of a headline novelty claim that then died:

[source: receipts/idiolect-trap-case-study.md]
The project's own diagnosis, recorded identically at each instance: *our invented vocabulary hid the occupation. The lesson is not "search harder" — it is search in the field's vocabulary, not ours, and before the program, not after.* It ran that sweep too late three times running.

What day-one field vocabulary would have *saved* — different scoping, earlier kills, redirected effort — is a counterfactual, and it is stated here as a hypothesis, not a measurement.

**Corroborating observations** (mappings *within* those three episodes — **not** eight independent replications):

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
**Two lessons from that table, both load-bearing:**
1. **The knowledge was in the weights the whole time.** A correctly-briefed refuter found Clemen & Winkler in five minutes. This is a **workflow bug, not a capability ceiling**.
2. **Every kill that was checked had a real quote and a defective citation** (2-for-2; Bröcker was the exception that held). **Subagents get the *idea* right and the *provenance* wrong** — wrong venue, dropped qualifiers, truncation exactly where the source turns against the claim. Hence §8.

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
1. **Naming is cheap and happens early.** Coining crisp project-local terms aids internal communication, and crispness feels like understanding. By definition the coinage diverges from the field's name — if you knew the field's name, you would have used it.
2. **The idiolect colonizes every artifact** — state docs, digests, prompts. Then: **every agent spawned from those artifacts is socialized into the idiolect at spawn time.** A human collaborator brings their own vocabulary and asks "isn't this just Murphy decomposition?" — the fresh-eyes function. An agent's entire worldview arrives via your prompt. **The onboarding context that makes agents useful is what destroys their independence for novelty checks.**
3. **Shared context is a hidden correlator** stacked on shared training corpus. N agents briefed from the same docs are one searcher with variance. (`feedback_fanout_agreement_not_independence`.) Demonstrated live on 2026-07-16: agents 1–3, all searching post-2023 arXiv in overlapping vocabulary, **converged** on "the identifiability framing survives" — and the convergence was worth nothing, because a refuter tasked to hunt pre-LLM literature killed their survivor. (The *specific* 1991 citation that refuter used later proved defective — see the withdrawn row above — which only sharpens the point: convergence was worthless **and** the thing that broke it needed verifying too.)
4. **Models mirror your ontology rather than correct it.** Nothing in the interaction rewards an agent for volunteering the field's name unprompted. Sycophancy at the ontology level.
5. **Novelty is an absence claim** — exactly where convergence is worth nothing. So the null comes back **confident**.

Any of: a novelty or "is this new?" claim · entering a research programme or direction · a grant/competition entry · a public artifact asserting a contribution · coining a term for a phenomenon you intend to build on.

### 2. Two frozen passes — never one

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
- **Pass A — blind naming/refutation.** The agent gets **only** the phenomenon restated in plain mathematical/operational language. **No project docs, no project vocabulary, no candidate terms, no prior conclusions, no preferred fields.** Ask it to name the concept and its owners cold. Freeze and archive this brief verbatim — it is the evidence that the pass was blind.
- **Pass B — verification and citation-walk.** *This* pass may receive Pass A's candidates, prior sweeps, and adjacent papers, and is tasked to verify against primary text and to citation-walk backward.

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
#### ⚠ Blindness is a TOOL-LEVEL control, not a prompt-level one (measured 2026-07-16)

#### The disjointness result — why 3 cross-model draws, not 5 same-briefed agents (measured 2026-07-16)

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
Consequences, both load-bearing:
- **The union is the payload; the agreement count is noise.** Pass B's five same-briefed agents produced *one* union. Three blind cross-model draws produced three.
- **A single draw's null is worthless, and so is a single draw's hit.** The one *correct* pre-registered prediction that session (reliability engineering as the general antecedent → Knight & Leveson) was found by **1 of 3** draws. Running only the other two would have scored a right prediction as wrong.
- **Pre-register predictions before any run returns.** 4 of 5 were wrong or mis-scoped; the blind draws beat the orchestrator's socialized priors. Sealed predictions are what makes that visible instead of retrofittable.

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
Not *"search for prior art on X"* — that returns comfortable nulls. Instead: **"What is this called? In which fields does it live? Who owns it? Name the oldest treatments you would expect to exist."** This forces **positive, checkable claims** where a null cannot hide, and elicits from weights before touching the web.

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
**Date rule: at least one source published before 2015, with no lower bound.** (Kelley 1927 and Blackwell 1953 both matter here; a "1950–2015 window" would wrongly exclude the former.) Old literature is reached by **graph-walking from a hit**, not keyword search — modern surfaces bury it. Every minelit scoop was pre-LLM: 1961, 1985, 2008, plus 1953/1973/1974/2001 in the 07-16 sweep.

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
**If the claim can be stated without mentioning LLMs, expect a pre-2015 owner.** Generality equals age: a threshold on a score, information garbling, expert aggregation, correlated evidence — all owned decades ago.

### 8. Verify the kills — the mirror failure

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
- Read the load-bearing citation's **primary text yourself**. Check the **venue** actually matches (the withdrawn Wilson row was a software manual dressed as *Psychometrika*).
- **Read past the ellipsis.** Truncation lands exactly where the source turns against the claim.
- **Check for the authors' own hedges** before reporting a result as settled (Rowe & Wright disowned the strong reading their quotes were used for).
- Conflicting IDs for one paper across agents = **hallucination signature**; report the conflict, don't pick one.
- A small model's "NOT FOUND" on a math PDF is **worth nothing** — it returned false negatives on all three real Bröcker quotes.
- **★ A snippet that states your conclusion in perfect words is a fabrication signal, not a find.** Measured **2-for-2 in the 07-16 patent sweep**: a search engine attributed to gwern's "Internet Search Tips" a near-perfect statement of the loop's search half — **not on the page** (grep returned zero); and attributed *"patent applicants use vague and abstract terms…"* to Lupu & Hanbury 2013 — the 99-page survey has **zero** occurrences of "vague"/"obfuscat"/"intentional." Both read as exactly what the searcher hoped for; both were inventions. **When a quote lands too cleanly on your thesis, that is the moment to open the primary, not to celebrate.**

[source: receipts/novelty-protocol.md]
- **An aggregator's "closed / not open access" is NOT evidence of absence.** OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar both reported **Larsen & Bong 2016 CLOSED** — it was freely archived; Unpaywall reported **Alcácer & Gittelman 2006** as `oa_status: closed, oa_locations: []` — a live green copy existed. Two aggregators, two wrong "closed"s, one session. Treat their negative as unchecked.
- **The Wayback CDX index of the authors' OWN uploads is the best route.** `safefetch "http://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=<host/path>*&output=text&fl=original,timestamp,statuscode,mimetype&limit=40"`, then fetch the original bytes via `https://web.archive.org/web/<timestamp>id_/<original-url>`. ResearchGate/academia/SSRN uploads are frequently archived there even when the live host is Cloudflare-walled. **This is how both Larsen & Bong (author's RG upload) and Alcácer (a now-dead host's green copy) were finally read.**
- **A repository's "full text" link can be a cover sheet.** UNIMAS's advertised full text of Larsen & Bong was a **2-page** ResearchGate cover sheet + p.529 — which is why every route "almost" worked. **Check the page count before believing you have the paper.**
- **Crossref exposes direct publisher PDF paths** search engines don't surface: `https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=...&rows=3&mailto=<you>`.
- **Fetch mechanics:** `safefetch`'s renderer refuses raw PDFs; use `wget` + `pdftotext -layout` (write the script and run via `saferun -f` — a compound `wget && pdftotext` line is denied). `r.jina.ai/<url>` extracts some PDFs the renderer won't, but blocks `web.archive.org`. **The PDF's internal metadata title can confirm provenance** — Larsen & Bong's read `Round 5. INN Synonymy 20151217_Accepted`, i.e. the accepted manuscript.
- **Scanned/typeset article PDFs often have a near-empty text layer** (the UNIMAS cover sheet extracted to 6k chars of nothing). A `pdftotext` "not found" on such a file is a false negative — read the pages visually with the Read tool's `pages` before concluding anything is absent.
